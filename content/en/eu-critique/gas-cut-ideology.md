# The Gas Cut: When Energy Policy Becomes Ideology

*worldlegalservice.com — EU Policy Critique Series, Article 3*

---

In March 2022, the EU committed in the Versailles Declaration to phase out European dependency on Russian fossil fuels as rapidly as possible. The commitment was framed as a response to the Russian invasion of Ukraine — a necessary severing of an economic relationship that had funded the aggressor and made Europe strategically vulnerable.

By December 2025, the European Parliament had voted 500 to 120 to approve a gradual ban on Russian natural gas imports, with full implementation by late 2027.

The policy is now settled law. The debate about whether it was the right policy — conducted honestly, with full acknowledgment of its costs and its alternatives — was never adequately had.

This article attempts to have part of that debate.

---

## What Europe Gave Up

Russia was the European Union's largest external supplier of natural gas at the time of the 2022 commitment. In some member states — Germany most prominently, but also Austria, Hungary, Slovakia, and several Central and Eastern European countries — Russian gas accounted for the majority of natural gas supply, which in turn powered significant portions of industrial production, home heating, and electricity generation.

The decision to cut this supply was presented as primarily a security and solidarity decision — the right thing to do regardless of economic cost. The economic cost was real and substantial.

European energy prices surged to historic levels in 2022. Industrial production in energy-intensive sectors — chemicals, steel, glass, ceramics, fertilizers — contracted sharply across Germany, the Netherlands, and other heavily industrialised member states. Energy poverty increased measurably in lower-income households across the continent. The economic costs of the energy transition were distributed unevenly — heavily on industrial workers and lower-income households, less heavily on the professional classes and the political decision-makers who announced the policy.

These costs were acknowledged in EU communication, usually briefly, usually followed by reference to the energy transition as a necessary long-term investment. The gap between the acknowledged costs and the sustained scrutiny those costs deserved was significant.

---

## The Strategic Logic Examined

The strategic justification for cutting Russian gas rested on two claims: that continued gas purchases funded Russian military operations, and that energy dependency created a strategic vulnerability that Russia could exploit.

Both claims have merit. Both claims also have complications that were insufficiently examined in the political debate.

**On funding military operations:** Russian energy revenues declined following the European gas cut, but Russia adapted through redirected exports to China and India, currency controls, and domestic fiscal adjustment. The relationship between European gas purchases and Russian military capacity was real but not as direct or as severable as the political framing suggested. The war in Ukraine has continued and been funded regardless.

**On strategic vulnerability:** Europe's energy dependency on Russia was a genuine vulnerability — one that had been identified by analysts for decades and ignored by successive European governments who prioritised cheap energy over strategic diversification. The vulnerability was real. The question of whether cutting gas imports abruptly, without adequate alternative supply secured in advance, was the optimal response to that vulnerability — versus a managed transition over a longer timeframe — was a legitimate policy question that received less rigorous public debate than it deserved.

The alternative supply that replaced Russian gas was primarily liquefied natural gas from the United States and Qatar — at significantly higher prices, with significantly higher transportation energy costs, and with its own geopolitical dependencies. Europe solved one dependency by creating others, at greater expense, with the costs passed to European consumers and industries.

---

## The Hungary Question

Hungary's refusal to fully comply with EU energy sanctions against Russia has been consistently framed in EU and Western European media as political obstruction — a Orbán government preference for Russian relationships over European solidarity, driven by ideology or by corruption or by both.

This framing may be accurate. It may also be incomplete.

Hungary is a landlocked Central European country whose energy infrastructure was built over decades around Russian supply. The practical cost of rapid transition away from Russian gas is higher for Hungary than for Germany, which has Atlantic coast access and far greater capital for infrastructure transition. The political economy of energy transition in a lower-income member state with limited fiscal space is materially different from the political economy in a wealthy Western European economy.

This does not make Hungary's position correct. It does suggest that the EU's insistence on uniform application of a policy whose costs are distributed very unevenly across member states — without adequate compensation mechanisms or transition support for those most affected — is a governance failure as much as it is a solidarity question.

The framing of "obstruction" conveniently locates the failure at the member state level rather than at the EU institutional level, where the decision to impose a uniformly costly policy without adequate differentiation was made.

---

## The Industrial Consequences

The energy price shock that followed the Russian gas cut contributed significantly to what has since become recognised as a structural competitiveness crisis in European industry.

Mario Draghi's 2024 report on European competitiveness — commissioned by the Commission and received with considerable political discomfort — documented the widening gap between European and American industrial energy costs as a major driver of European competitiveness decline. European industrial electricity prices have been persistently two to three times higher than US prices, with the gas price shock of 2022-2023 accelerating a divergence that was already underway.

The consequence has been real and measurable: investment in energy-intensive industries has shifted away from Europe, toward the United States and Asia. Facilities have closed. Jobs have moved. The communities whose economic base depended on industries that cannot survive European energy prices have not been adequately compensated — through industrial policy, through transition support, through the kind of sustained public investment that the scale of the disruption required.

The EU has acknowledged the competitiveness problem. It has not adequately acknowledged the connection between the gas cut decision and the competitiveness crisis — which would require admitting that a policy decision with geopolitical justification produced economic consequences that were foreseeable, that fell disproportionately on working people, and that were managed inadequately.

---

## What This Is Not

This analysis is not an argument that Europe should have continued buying Russian gas indefinitely. The strategic vulnerability was real. The funding of Russian state revenues through energy purchases raised genuine ethical questions. The long-term case for European energy independence is sound.

It is an argument that the decision deserved — and did not receive — the kind of honest cost-benefit analysis and democratic deliberation that a policy with consequences of this magnitude requires.

It is an argument that the citizens who paid the costs of the policy — in higher energy bills, in industrial job losses, in reduced economic competitiveness — deserved honest communication about the trade-offs involved, rather than the framing of "necessary sacrifice" that made political accountability for the costs difficult to assign.

And it is an argument that energy policy — which determines the material conditions of daily life for hundreds of millions of people — is precisely the kind of policy that should not be made primarily through geopolitical logic applied by technocratic institutions with limited democratic accountability.

The gas cut may have been right. The process that produced it was not.

---

## The Pattern

What the gas cut shares with the other EU policy failures examined in this series is a consistent pattern: a decision made rapidly, under conditions of urgency, by institutions with limited democratic accountability, with costs distributed unevenly across the population, with the justification framed in terms that make democratic challenge difficult, and with the accountability for outcomes structured so that no one bears meaningful responsibility for what goes wrong.

This pattern is not specific to any single policy. It is the output of an institutional architecture that was designed to make certain decisions quickly, over democratic objection if necessary, in the name of a European interest that the institutions themselves define.

The gas cut is a case study in what that architecture produces when the urgency is real, the geopolitical framing is sincere, and the costs are still borne disproportionately by people who had no meaningful voice in the decision.
