# Migration Without Integration: The Cost of a Policy That Serves Nobody

*worldlegalservice.com — EU Policy Critique Series, Article 4*

---

European migration policy has managed, over the past decade, to fail almost everyone it touches — the migrants who arrive in conditions of danger and uncertainty, the communities that receive them without adequate support, the asylum systems that have collapsed under demand they were never designed to handle, and the broader European political project, which has been destabilised more thoroughly by migration than by any other single policy failure.

This is a significant achievement in dysfunction. It requires sustained institutional failure at multiple levels simultaneously.

Understanding how it happened — and why the institutional response has consistently produced more of the same — requires looking past the political narratives that dominate the public debate, in both directions.

---

## The Numbers in Context

The European migration debate is frequently conducted with poor statistical literacy on all sides. The scale of irregular migration to Europe — real and significant — is often exaggerated in political discourse in ways that make rational policy response more difficult. At the same time, the genuine integration challenges created by large-scale migration of people without the language, credentials, or economic networks to participate in European labor markets are often minimised by those for whom acknowledging them feels like conceding ground to nativist politics.

Both distortions serve the interests of political factions rather than the interests of the people affected.

What is clear from the data is this: the European Union has received, over the past decade, several million people whose legal status, integration prospects, and economic participation have been managed with insufficient capacity, insufficient coordination, and insufficient honesty about what successful integration actually requires.

A significant proportion of arrivals in the 2015-2016 peak and subsequent years were working-age men from countries experiencing conflict or economic instability — a demographic whose integration into European labor markets requires language acquisition, credential recognition, vocational training, and sustained institutional support over periods of years. In many receiving countries, this support was not systematically provided. The result — visible in housing estates, urban peripheries, and labor market statistics across several Northern and Western European countries — has been large communities of people who arrived years ago, are legally present, and remain economically marginal.

This outcome serves nobody. It does not serve the migrants, who sought a better life and found precarity. It does not serve the communities that host them, which bear the social costs of inadequate integration without the economic benefits that successful integration would produce. It does not serve the European political project, which has been handed a weapon by those who argue that migration is inherently destabilising.

---

## The Policy Architecture That Produced This

The EU's migration policy architecture is built around the Dublin Regulation — a framework that assigns responsibility for asylum claims to the first EU member state where an asylum seeker arrives. In theory, this prevents asylum shopping — the practice of moving between member states to find the most favorable conditions. In practice, it places the burden of first processing on the member states with the most accessible external borders — Italy, Greece, Spain — regardless of whether those states have the capacity, infrastructure, or political will to provide adequate reception and processing.

The Dublin framework has been universally acknowledged as inadequate for years. It has produced predictable consequences: overwhelmed reception systems in frontline states, secondary movements of migrants across internal EU borders in violation of the rules, and political crises in receiving countries that have periodically threatened to collapse both national governments and EU solidarity.

The EU's response to this acknowledged failure has been a series of reform attempts that have stalled at the Council level — where the member states most affected by migration pressure and the member states most resistant to mandatory relocation mechanisms have been unable to reach agreement. The New Pact on Migration and Asylum, agreed in 2024, represents the most recent attempt at a comprehensive framework. Whether it will perform better than its predecessors in practice remains to be seen.

## The Integration Failure

The EU's migration policy debate has focused predominantly on border management, asylum processing, and refugee status determination — the questions of who is allowed to come and how their legal status is determined. It has given comparatively little sustained attention to the question that ultimately determines whether migration is a social benefit or a social cost: **what happens to people after they arrive?**

Successful integration requires, at minimum: language acquisition at a functional level; recognition or re-qualification of professional credentials; housing that is not segregated from the broader community; education access for children that does not create parallel systems; and labor market access that is not blocked by discrimination, credential barriers, or welfare structures that inadvertently make employment economically irrational.

These requirements are resource-intensive. They take years to deliver outcomes. The political payoff is long-term and diffuse — a well-integrated migrant community contributing to the labor market and tax base a decade from now does not help the government that invested in integration today. The political cost of integration failures, on the other hand, is immediate and concentrated — visible in crime statistics, welfare expenditure, housing pressure, and the electoral support for parties that argue the entire project was misguided.

The structural incentive is therefore to underinvest in integration — to accept migrants into legal status, provide minimum reception conditions, and leave the longer-term integration to market forces and community resourcefulness. This is what has happened in many EU member states. The results are visible.

## The Taxpayer Question

The political debate about migration frequently frames the resource question in terms of what migrants cost the welfare state — a framing that generates heat without light, because the answer depends entirely on the timeframe of analysis and the success of integration.

A migrant who arrives, is housed at public expense, receives welfare support for two years, acquires language and credentials, enters the labor market, and contributes taxes for the following thirty years is a net fiscal positive — substantially so, given Europe's demographic challenge of an aging population and shrinking workforce.

A migrant who arrives, is housed at public expense, receives welfare support for five years, never adequately learns the language, never gains recognized credentials, remains economically inactive, and remains a permanent charge on public resources is a net fiscal negative.

The difference between these two outcomes is almost entirely determined by the quality and adequacy of integration policy — by whether public institutions invest the resources and sustained attention that the second outcome requires to be avoided.

Europe has spent enormous political energy debating whether to accept migrants. It has spent inadequate institutional energy and resources on the question of what to do with them once they arrive to ensure that both the migrants and the receiving societies benefit from the relationship.

The result — large numbers of people in legal limbo or marginal economic status, supported by taxpayer resources without producing the economic participation that would justify that support and eventually exceed it — is precisely what inadequate integration policy produces. It is a policy failure, not an inherent consequence of migration.

## Who Benefits from the Current System

If the current migration policy system fails migrants, receiving communities, and the European project, the question worth asking is: who benefits from its continuation?

The answer is not simple, but it includes some actors whose interests have received insufficient scrutiny.

**Informal labor markets** in agriculture, construction, cleaning, and domestic service benefit from large populations of people in precarious legal status, dependent on informal employment because formal channels are blocked. This labor is cheap, flexible, and unlikely to organise. The employers who depend on it have no interest in integration that would give workers greater labor market options and bargaining power.

**Political entrepreneurs** on both sides of the migration debate benefit from its continuation as an unresolved crisis. A migration system that was working — that processed claims efficiently, integrated arrivals successfully, and produced documented economic benefits — would remove a major source of political mobilisation for both nativist parties and the liberal coalition that defines itself against them.

**Smuggling networks** that charge migrants thousands of euros for dangerous passage across the Mediterranean or through Balkan routes benefit from the absence of legal migration pathways. Every legal channel that is closed or made more restrictive increases demand for their services.

None of these actors have formal power over EU migration policy. All of them benefit from its continued dysfunction.

## What an Honest Policy Would Look Like

An honest European migration policy would begin with a frank acknowledgment that Europe will continue to receive migrants — from demographic pressure in Africa and the Middle East, from climate displacement, from conflict — regardless of how hostile the border regime becomes. The question is not whether to have migration but how to manage it in ways that produce outcomes better than the current ones for everyone involved.

That honest policy would include:

**Legal pathways that match labor market needs.** Europe has significant labor shortages in specific sectors and specific skill levels. Migration policy that channels people toward those shortages — through labor migration agreements, skills-matching programs, and credential recognition — produces better outcomes than a system that admits people on humanitarian grounds and then fails to connect them to the economy.

**Mandatory, adequately funded integration programs.** Language acquisition, credential assessment, employment support, and housing that enables rather than prevents integration should be legal requirements attached to legal status — not optional services provided at inadequate scale. The cost of these programs should be understood as an investment against the much larger cost of long-term economic marginalisation.

**Equitable burden-sharing within the EU.** The Dublin framework's allocation of responsibility to frontline states is unsustainable and unjust. Genuine burden-sharing — whether through mandatory relocation, financial compensation to frontline states, or shared processing infrastructure — is a prerequisite for a functional common policy.

**Honest evaluation of outcomes.** Migration policy should be subject to the same outcome measurement framework proposed in this series for all major EU policy commitments: pre-defined objectives, independent assessment, public reporting, and accountability for failures.

---

## The Honest Conclusion

Migration policy is perhaps the most politically charged area of EU governance — the one most vulnerable to the kind of narrative distortion that makes honest analysis difficult and honest policy nearly impossible.

The honest conclusion is unglamorous: European migration policy has failed not because migration is inherently unmanageable but because the institutions responsible for managing it have consistently underinvested in the hard, slow, resource-intensive work of integration, have failed to build the burden-sharing mechanisms that a common policy requires, and have allowed the political dynamics of the migration debate to substitute for the policy analysis that would improve outcomes.

The people who pay the price for this failure include migrants living in precarity, communities managing integration without adequate support, and taxpayers funding a system that produces worse outcomes than a better-designed alternative would at comparable or lower cost.

They deserve better than the policy they have. So far, they have not received it.
