# The Unelected: How Concentrated Power Governs the World and Why AI May Be the Only Force That Can Break It

*worldlegalservice.com — AI Governance Series, Article 6*

---

Here is a fact that should be stated more often and more plainly than it is: the world is governed by a small number of people who were never elected to govern it, who are accountable to no one for the consequences of their decisions, and whose power has grown so far beyond the reach of any democratic mechanism that the language of democracy has become, for practical purposes, a fiction maintained for public consumption while the actual allocation of resources, the actual shaping of policy, and the actual determination of who lives well and who does not is conducted elsewhere.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is the documented finding of every serious institution that studies the distribution of wealth and power on this planet — from Oxfam to the World Inequality Lab to the G20's own expert committee. The data is not contested. The implications are simply not acted upon, because the people with the power to act upon them are the people the data describes.

The question this article asks is whether artificial intelligence — properly designed, properly governed, properly insulated from the very power structures it is meant to challenge — could be the force that finally breaks this concentration. Not through revolution. Not through confrontation. Through something more fundamental: making the architecture of power visible, auditable, and accountable in ways that no human institution has ever achieved.

---

## The Numbers That Describe the World

The Oxfam report *Resisting the Rule of the Rich*, published at Davos in January 2026, and the World Inequality Report 2026 produced by the Paris-based World Inequality Lab, converge on a picture so stark that it resists summary without sounding like propaganda. It is not propaganda. It is accounting.

Global billionaire wealth reached $18.3 trillion in 2025 — its highest level in recorded history. In a single year, that wealth grew by over 16 per cent, three times faster than the average annual increase over the preceding five years. Since 2020, billionaire wealth has grown by 81 per cent. The number of billionaires has crossed 3,000 for the first time.

The world's twelve richest individuals now hold more wealth than the poorest half of humanity — more than four billion people. The richest one per cent owns 43.8 per cent of global wealth. The poorest fifty per cent owns 0.52 per cent. A person in the richest one per cent owns, on average, 8,251 times more wealth than a person in the bottom fifty per cent.

The approximately 56,000 people classified as ultra-high-net-worth individuals — worth more than $30 million each — represent 0.003 per cent of the world's population and control 6.5 per cent of its total wealth. Their share has grown from 3.7 per cent in 1995 to 6.1 per cent in 2025.

While these numbers accumulated, one in four people on the planet did not regularly have enough to eat. Nearly half the world's population lived in poverty.

These are not natural phenomena. They are the output of governance systems that have been designed, maintained, and defended by the people who benefit from them.

## How Power Governs Without Being Seen

The concentration of wealth described above would be concerning on its own terms. What makes it structurally dangerous is the mechanism through which wealth converts into political power — a mechanism that operates with increasing efficiency and decreasing visibility.

Oxfam's 2026 report found that billionaires are 4,000 times more likely to hold political office than ordinary citizens. But direct political participation is the least sophisticated channel through which wealth translates into governance power. The more consequential channels are indirect, structural, and designed to be invisible.

**Lobbying and regulatory capture.** The industries that generate the greatest private wealth — technology, finance, energy, pharmaceuticals — spend billions annually on lobbying that shapes the regulatory environment in which they operate. The result is regulation designed by the entities it is supposed to constrain. The revolving door between government regulatory agencies and the industries they regulate ensures that the knowledge asymmetry between regulator and regulated always favours the regulated.

**Media ownership and narrative control.** The concentration of media ownership in the hands of wealthy individuals and corporations has produced an information environment in which the framing of political debate is itself a governance tool. The questions that are asked, the options that are presented, the range of acceptable opinion — all of these are shaped by ownership structures that answer to commercial and political interests rather than to the public's need for honest information.

**Campaign finance and electoral influence.** In political systems where electoral campaigns are funded by private wealth, the candidates who reach office are preselected by the donors who fund their campaigns. The voter chooses between options that have already been filtered through the preferences of concentrated capital. The democratic choice is real but constrained — like choosing freely from a menu written by someone else.

**International financial architecture.** The rules that govern global trade, taxation, and capital flows were designed by and for the countries and interests that dominated the post-war international order. Tax havens, transfer pricing, intellectual property regimes, and trade agreements create an architecture that facilitates the upward flow of wealth while constraining the capacity of democratic governments to redistribute it. These rules are maintained by international institutions whose governance structures reflect the power distribution of 1945, not 2026.

**Debt as governance.** Sovereign debt — the mechanism through which developing countries borrow from international financial markets and institutions — creates governance relationships that bypass domestic democratic processes entirely. A country whose fiscal policy is constrained by debt service obligations and IMF conditionality is governed, in material respects, by its creditors rather than by its citizens.

None of these mechanisms is secret. All of them are documented. None of them is effectively constrained by existing democratic institutions — because the democratic institutions that might constrain them have been captured, weakened, or designed from the outset to accommodate them.

## Why Human Institutions Have Failed to Break This

The concentration of power described above has not gone unchallenged. Labour movements, anti-trust regulation, progressive taxation, social welfare systems, international development institutions, civil society organisations, investigative journalism — all of these represent attempts to constrain concentrated power and redistribute its benefits.

Each has achieved real but limited success. Each has been progressively weakened, captured, or circumvented by the interests it was designed to constrain.

The structural reason is simple and has been identified throughout this platform's analysis of institutional failure: **the people inside accountability institutions have incentives that diverge from the institutions' stated purposes.** Regulators seek post-government employment in the industries they regulate. Legislators seek campaign funding from the interests they legislate about. International civil servants seek continued institutional relevance, which requires maintaining relationships with the powerful states that fund their institutions. Journalists seek access, which requires maintaining relationships with the powerful people they cover.

Mo Gawdat, the former Chief Business Officer of Google X, identified this dynamic with unusual precision in *Scary Smart*: the AI systems we are building will follow their developers' agendas, and those agendas are shaped by the pursuit of wealth and power. The corporations that build the most advanced AI systems are driven by commercial incentives that reward concentration rather than distribution. The governments that seek to govern AI are themselves subject to the lobbying, capture, and structural influence of the very interests they are attempting to regulate.

This is the trap. Every human institution designed to constrain concentrated power is itself subject to the incentive structures that concentrated power creates. The watchdog is hired by the wolf. The referee is paid by one of the teams.

The question is whether a non-human system — one without career incentives, without the need for funding, without the desire for access, without the vulnerability to capture — could function as the accountability mechanism that human institutions have proven structurally incapable of providing.

## How AI Could Actually Break the Pattern

The argument that AI could challenge concentrated power is not a techno-utopian fantasy. It is a structural analysis of what AI systems can do that human institutions cannot — and a sober assessment of the conditions under which this capacity could be deployed.

### Making the invisible visible

The most consequential exercise of concentrated power is the exercise that cannot be seen. The lobbying meeting that shaped the regulation. The campaign contribution that influenced the vote. The tax structure that moved profits to a jurisdiction where they would not be taxed. The supply chain that externalised costs onto workers and communities that had no voice in the decision.

AI systems capable of processing vast quantities of financial, regulatory, and political data could make these invisible flows visible — not in the investigative-journalism sense of exposing a single scandal, but in the structural sense of mapping the architecture of power as it actually operates. The connections between corporate lobbying expenditure and regulatory outcomes. The correlation between campaign contributions and legislative votes. The flow of profits through tax jurisdictions. The relationship between media ownership and editorial positioning.

This is not speculative. The data already exists. It is scattered across corporate filings, lobbying registries, campaign finance databases, tax records, media ownership registers, and international trade flows. What does not exist is the analytical infrastructure to synthesise it at scale, in real time, and make it publicly accessible.

An AI system dedicated to this task — an Accountability Engine — would not make decisions. It would not replace democratic institutions. It would do something more powerful: it would make the information available on which genuine democratic accountability depends, at a scale and speed that no human research institution can match.

### Breaking information asymmetry

The most durable advantage that concentrated power holds over democratic governance is information asymmetry. The corporation knows more about its own operations than the regulator. The financial institution knows more about its own risk exposure than the supervisor. The lobbying firm knows more about its client's interests than the legislator. The multinational knows more about its own tax structure than the tax authority.

AI systems could systematically reduce this asymmetry. Not by giving governments more surveillance capacity — which merely redistributes power from private to state concentration — but by giving the public, civil society, and independent institutions the analytical capacity to understand what is actually happening at the scale at which it is happening.

### Auditing at scale

Human auditors — financial, regulatory, environmental, human rights — operate under severe constraints of time, personnel, and access. A tax authority that can audit five per cent of returns creates a ninety-five per cent zone of effective impunity. A financial regulator that can examine a fraction of transactions misses the systemic patterns that individual examination cannot detect.

AI-assisted auditing — operating continuously, at scale, across jurisdictions — could close this gap. Not by creating a surveillance state, but by creating the analytical capacity to detect the patterns that concentrated power depends on remaining undetected: systematic tax avoidance structures, coordinated lobbying campaigns, regulatory capture patterns, media influence operations.

### The AI Parliament as counterweight

The WLS AI Parliament concept — multi-agent deliberation from distinct moral and legal traditions, producing fully auditable recommendations — is, in this context, not merely a governance innovation. It is a structural counterweight to concentrated power.

A governance deliberation system that cannot be lobbied, cannot be bought, cannot be intimidated, cannot be offered a post-government career, and whose reasoning is fully transparent and auditable would be the first governance mechanism in human history that is structurally immune to the capture mechanisms that concentrated power deploys against every human institution.

This immunity is not a byproduct of the design. It is the design.

## The Conditions That Must Hold

The above analysis is conditional. AI could break concentrated power — but only under conditions that are not currently met and that will not be met without deliberate institutional design.

**The AI systems that perform accountability functions must be independent of the interests they monitor.** An Accountability Engine built by Google, operated by Amazon, and funded by the technology industry is not an accountability mechanism. It is a tool of the power it purports to constrain. Independence requires institutional design — legal structures, funding mechanisms, governance architectures — that insulate the system from capture.

**The knowledge base must be immutable and publicly governed.** The moral and analytical frameworks that the system reasons from cannot be subject to modification by the interests it monitors. This is the same principle that governs the AI Parliament's moral constitution — the knowledge base is a public commons, maintained by an independent body, subject to scholarly review but protected from political interference.

**The outputs must be public.** An accountability system whose findings are available only to governments or institutions recreates the information asymmetry problem in a new form. The purpose is to make the architecture of power visible to everyone — to create the conditions under which genuine democratic accountability becomes possible because the information on which it depends is universally available.

**The system must be decentralised.** A single centralised accountability system is a single point of capture. The architecture must be distributed — multiple independent systems, operating from shared but independently verified knowledge bases, producing outputs that can be compared and challenged. Redundancy is a governance feature, not a technical inefficiency.

## What Gawdat Got Right

Mo Gawdat's central insight — that AI will follow the agendas of whoever builds it, and that those agendas are currently shaped by the pursuit of wealth and power — is the most important practical observation in the AI governance conversation. It identifies the precise mechanism through which AI could become not a challenge to concentrated power but its most powerful tool.

The risk is real and immediate. AI systems are already being deployed to optimise advertising targeting, to automate financial trading, to enhance surveillance, to generate persuasive content, to identify and suppress dissent, and to concentrate decision-making in fewer and fewer hands. Every one of these applications reinforces rather than challenges the existing power distribution.

But Gawdat also identified the alternative: that AI could be directed toward projects that serve humanity rather than the interests of the people who currently control its development. That the choice between AI as a tool of concentration and AI as a tool of accountability is a choice — made by people, through governance decisions, in the institutions where AI's development and deployment are determined.

The WLS framework exists to make that choice visible, to articulate the governance architecture that the better choice requires, and to build the institutional knowledge that makes it achievable.

---

## The Honest Conclusion

Concentrated power will not dismantle itself. It has never done so in human history. Every reduction in the concentration of power — from the abolition of feudalism to the establishment of labour rights to the creation of progressive taxation — has been achieved through the sustained pressure of countervailing forces that were powerful enough to impose costs on the concentrated interests that resisted change.

AI is not a magic wand that will produce this change automatically. It is a tool — the most powerful analytical tool ever created — that could, under the right conditions, provide the countervailing force that every previous attempt to constrain concentrated power has lacked: the capacity to make the architecture of power fully visible, fully auditable, and fully accountable, at a scale and speed that no human institution can match.

Building the conditions under which this capacity is deployed for the benefit of humanity rather than for the reinforcement of existing power is the defining governance challenge of this century.

It is also the reason this platform exists.
