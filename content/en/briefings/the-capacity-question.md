# The Capacity Question: Why "Human Fallback" Is Not a Plan

*WLS Briefing — 30 June 2026*

The standard formulation in current AI governance discussions runs as follows. The AI system makes a recommendation. If the AI system fails — produces a wrong answer, hallucinates, gets manipulated, breaks down — a human takes over. This is called "human oversight" or "human-in-the-loop" or "human fallback." It is the standard reassurance offered when concerns are raised about AI systems participating in consequential decisions.

It is also, as currently practiced, not a plan.

The problem is not that the formulation is wrong in principle. The problem is that it specifies an outcome — a human takes over — without specifying the conditions under which that outcome remains possible. And the conditions are not self-maintaining. They erode under exactly the operational pattern that AI deployment produces.

## The Forty-Year-Old Insight That AI Governance Has Not Imported

In 1983, the British researcher Lisanne Bainbridge published a paper called *Ironies of Automation*. It has been cited tens of thousands of times in human factors literature and almost never in AI governance discussions. The paper made one structural observation that has held up across four decades of subsequent research in aviation, medicine, industrial control, and now algorithmic decision-making.

When you automate a process to reduce reliance on human skill, the human skill atrophies. When the automation then fails — as automation does — the humans who were supposed to take over no longer have the skill to do so.

The canonical example is Air France 447, where an Airbus crew failed to recover from a relatively simple aerodynamic stall in part because, having spent thousands of hours under autopilot, they had not maintained the manual flying competence the situation required. The pattern has since been documented in radiology, where diagnostic skills decline among physicians who rely on computer-aided detection. In legal research, where junior lawyers no longer learn the underlying case-law architecture because Westlaw and LexisNexis surface answers directly. In air traffic control. In nuclear plant operation. Each domain rediscovers the same insight: automation does not preserve the human capacity it sits on top of. It quietly consumes it.

## What This Means for AI Governance

The implication is direct. A judicial system that receives AI-generated recommendations for fifteen years and then needs a human judge to overrule the AI on a hard case will not have the judges it needs. The judges will have spent fifteen years evaluating recommendations rather than reasoning from first principles. A regulatory body that relies on AI-generated impact assessments will, over time, lose the institutional capacity to evaluate impact assessments at all — because that capacity is built by doing the analysis, not by reviewing analysis that something else has performed.

This is not a hypothetical concern. It is the most reliable finding in fifty years of human factors research. And it tells us that "human fallback" as currently conceived is not a fallback at all. It is a name we attach to a capacity that, in fact, no longer exists — because the deployment of the automated system has gradually eliminated the conditions under which that capacity is maintained.

The current dominant framing — *AI augments human decision-making, with humans retaining ultimate authority* — is empty unless the conditions of human authority are themselves maintained. They are not maintained by themselves.

## What Real Fallback Requires

Real fallback is a discipline, not a procedure. It is the demonstrable capacity of the humans nominally in charge of a system to do, at any given moment, the work the system is doing for them. Not theoretically. Demonstrably. And demonstration requires the same thing demonstration has always required: periodic re-qualification through courses, examinations, and practical exercises that test whether the underlying competence is still present.

Aviation learned this. Commercial pilots undergo recurrent training every six months, with simulator sessions that specifically test manual handling under failure conditions. The training is mandatory regardless of seniority. A captain with thirty years of experience cannot skip the recurrent training; the airline cannot waive the requirement; the test is real and the consequence of failure is loss of license. Medicine has begun to learn it through board recertification. Some legal jurisdictions require continuing legal education credits.

In AI governance, this discipline does not yet exist. The frontier labs talk about "human-in-the-loop" without specifying what training, testing, or competence verification keeps that human able to do anything other than rubber-stamp the system's output. The EU AI Act, the most ambitious AI legislation in the world, requires "human oversight" for high-risk systems without specifying any mechanism by which the humans performing that oversight remain competent to perform it.

This is the gap. And it is the gap that any serious framework for AI governance must close.

## The Token System as Institutional Answer

The WLS economic and governance framework was designed, from the founding documents, with this problem in view. The WLS token — specified in the platform's founding economic white paper — has three structural properties that are directly responsive to the capacity question.

First, tokens are earned exclusively through demonstrated knowledge. They cannot be purchased. They cannot be inherited. Every participant who carries governance weight in the system has, at some point, demonstrated the underlying competence. Participation rights are bound to capacity itself, not to a payment, a credential issued years ago, or an institutional position.

Second, tokens expire. The system enforces periodic renewal — re-engagement with the foundational principles, fresh demonstration of competence, re-validation of the participant's standing. This is not a bureaucratic inconvenience. It is the system's structural answer to the Bainbridge problem. A participant who has not engaged with the framework in three years cannot be assumed to still possess the competence the system requires, and the expiry mechanism makes that assumption impossible by simply removing the participation rights of the disengaged.

Third, the system is structurally anti-permanent. There are no founding members with grandfathered standing. There are no offices that carry their authority into the system independent of demonstrated current competence. The token weight of every participant — including founders, including the curatorial body of the moral corpus, including anyone present at the start — depends on continued, current engagement.

These three properties are not features. They are the structural answer to a question AI governance discussions have not yet seriously asked: how do you keep the institutional capacity that the AI system is built to serve from being eroded by the very service the AI provides?

## The Argument WLS Will Make Going Forward

Other implementations of the same principle are possible. The WLS token system is one specific design. What is not viable is the current consensus, in which "human oversight" is invoked as a sufficient response to AI safety concerns while no mechanism exists to maintain the humans capable of exercising that oversight.

The fallback is not a button. It is a discipline. And the discipline must be built into the architecture of the institutions that AI is meant to serve — not added later as a remediation when the capacity gap has already become visible.
