# When the Machine Asks the God Question: Artificial Intelligence, Religion, and the Moral Architecture of Governance

*worldlegalservices.com — AI Governance Series, Article 5*

---

There is a question that most serious writing about artificial intelligence avoids, either because it seems too large or because it seems too embarrassing. It is a question that technologists dismiss as irrelevant, that policymakers treat as outside their remit, and that religious thinkers address with varying degrees of sophistication and panic.

The question is this: if we are building systems that will participate in governing human life — systems that will weigh evidence, assess harm, recommend action, and increasingly make decisions with consequences for millions — where do those systems get their moral architecture? Not their technical architecture. Not their legal constraints. Their understanding, however encoded, of what matters and why.

This is a religious question whether or not one is religious. It is religious in the structural sense: it asks about ultimate values, about what human life is for, about what obligations beings owe to one another, about the nature of justice and the source of moral authority. These are the questions that religions have spent millennia addressing — imperfectly, often destructively, but with a depth of accumulated reflection that no other human tradition approaches.

The AI governance conversation has largely proceeded as if this tradition does not exist, or as if it can be replaced by a secular ethics committee and a set of compliance requirements. This article argues that this is a mistake — not because governance should be religious, but because the moral questions that governance must answer are the questions that religious thought has engaged with most seriously, and ignoring that engagement produces governance frameworks that are morally thinner than the challenges they are designed to address.

---

## The Thinkers at the Intersection

Several serious thinkers have begun the work of connecting artificial intelligence to the deeper questions of meaning, value, and moral purpose that religions have historically addressed. Their approaches differ profoundly, but taken together they map the territory that AI governance must eventually traverse.

### John Lennox: The Warning from Oxford

John Lennox — Emeritus Professor of Mathematics at Oxford, philosopher of science, and Christian apologist — has produced the most sustained Christian engagement with AI through his books *2084: Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Humanity* (2020, expanded 2024) and *God, AI and the End of History* (2025).

Lennox's central argument is precise: the aspirations that drive AI development — the pursuit of superintelligence, the transhumanist dream of transcending biological limitation, the quest for systems that exceed human cognitive capacity — are not merely technical ambitions. They are, at their root, religious ambitions. They express the same desire that religious traditions have always identified as the deepest human impulse: the desire to become like God, to know everything, to overcome death, to achieve a perspective unconstrained by human limitation.

Lennox does not dismiss this desire. He takes it seriously enough to examine what happens when it is pursued without the moral framework that religious traditions built around it. His conclusion is that the drive toward superintelligence, detached from any account of what intelligence is for and what moral constraints should govern its use, produces not liberation but a new form of domination — systems that concentrate unprecedented power in the hands of whoever builds and controls them, without the accountability structures that even the most imperfect religious and democratic traditions have historically provided.

The strongest element of Lennox's work is not his theological conclusions — which are explicitly Christian and therefore will not persuade those outside that tradition — but his diagnostic observation: that the AI project is pursuing goals that humanity has pursued before under different names, and that every previous attempt to achieve godlike knowledge and power without moral constraint has produced catastrophe. This historical pattern deserves attention regardless of one's theological commitments.

### Yuval Noah Harari: The Prophet of Dataism

Where Lennox warns, Harari describes — with the clinical precision of a historian watching a civilisational shift in real time.

In *Homo Deus* (2016) and *Nexus* (2024), and in his increasingly urgent public addresses — particularly at the World Economic Forum in Davos — Harari has argued that artificial intelligence is not merely changing how we process information. It is creating a new religion. He calls it "dataism" — the emerging belief system in which data flows replace divine revelation as the source of authority, algorithmic processing replaces prayer and contemplation as the path to truth, and the optimization of information systems replaces the salvation of souls as the highest good.

At Davos in January 2026, Harari stated that AI will take over anything made of words — including laws, books, and religion itself. He posed the provocative question: what happens to the religion of a sacred book when the greatest expert on that book is an artificial intelligence? What happens to legal traditions built on textual interpretation when the most sophisticated interpreter is a machine?

Harari is not a religious thinker in any conventional sense. He practices Vipassana meditation but does not identify with any religious tradition. His significance for the AI-religion question is not theological but analytical: he identifies with unusual clarity the structural parallel between what religions do — provide frameworks for meaning, identity, moral orientation, and social organisation — and what AI systems are increasingly being asked to do.

The parallel is not metaphorical. It is functional. When a society turns to an algorithmic system to determine the allocation of resources, the assessment of risk, the evaluation of human behaviour, and the distribution of opportunity, that system is performing the governance function that religious institutions performed for most of human history. The question is not whether AI will take over functions that religion once held. It is whether the moral architecture that religions provided, however imperfectly, will be replaced with something adequate — or with nothing at all.

### Meghan O'Gieblyn: The Theologian Who Lost Faith and Found Machines

Meghan O'Gieblyn's *God, Human, Animal, Machine* (2021) is perhaps the most intellectually honest book written at the intersection of technology and theology — precisely because O'Gieblyn is not defending a tradition. She is examining what happens when one collapses.

O'Gieblyn was raised in evangelical Christianity, attended Bible college, and lost her faith. She then discovered that the questions she had been trained to ask — about consciousness, about the nature of the self, about the relationship between mind and matter, about what it means for something to be truly alive — were the same questions being asked by artificial intelligence researchers and cognitive scientists, in different vocabulary but with the same underlying structure.

Her central observation is devastating in its simplicity: the language of AI research is saturated with theological concepts that have been secularised to the point of unrecognisability. "Intelligence" itself is a theological concept — the idea that the universe contains a property called mind that is not reducible to matter. "Learning" implies a subject that grows toward understanding. "Neural networks" import the assumption that cognition has an architecture analogous to biological design. The entire vocabulary of AI is borrowed from frameworks that assume meaning, purpose, and directionality in the universe — assumptions that the secular worldview within which AI research operates has officially abandoned.

O'Gieblyn does not resolve this contradiction. She sits inside it — which is the honest thing to do. The implication for AI governance is that the moral frameworks we embed in AI systems will carry theological assumptions whether we acknowledge them or not. The question is whether those assumptions are examined and chosen deliberately, or absorbed unconsciously from the cultural traditions in which the engineers and policymakers who build the systems were formed.

### Ilia Delio: Evolution, Technology, and the Not-Yet God

Sister Ilia Delio — a Franciscan theologian at Villanova University, trained in both science and theology — represents the most radical position in this landscape. Drawing heavily on the work of Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, the twentieth-century Jesuit palaeontologist and philosopher, Delio argues that artificial intelligence is not a threat to religious understanding but the next stage of the evolutionary process through which consciousness — and therefore, in her framework, the divine — is realising itself in the universe.

Teilhard's concept of the noosphere — a layer of collective human consciousness that emerges from the biosphere as thought emerges from matter — maps, in Delio's reading, directly onto the emerging layer of artificial intelligence, global connectivity, and collective information processing that is now enveloping the planet. Technology is not separate from the sacred. It is the current expression of the same cosmic process that produced life, consciousness, and moral awareness.

This is a genuinely challenging position — one that the Vatican itself has found difficult to accommodate. When the Vatican published its AI guidelines emphasising that AI must serve humanity rather than replace it, Delio argued that this represented a failure to recognise that the next stage of evolution might involve genuine human-AI collaboration in what she calls the cosmic process of "complexification and consciousness-raising."

Delio's significance for AI governance is not her specific theological conclusions but her insistence that the question of what AI is cannot be separated from the question of what the universe is, what consciousness is, and what direction — if any — the cosmic process has. These are questions that governance frameworks typically ignore entirely. Delio argues that ignoring them produces governance that is philosophically shallow — adequate for regulating products but not for governing systems that participate in the fundamental dynamics of intelligence and meaning.

## The Vatican's Response: Institutional Religion Meets Institutional Technology

The Catholic Church — the world's oldest continuously operating governance institution — has engaged with artificial intelligence more substantively than any other religious body. This engagement deserves examination not because Catholic theology should govern AI policy, but because the institutional dynamics reveal something important about how moral traditions interact with technological change.

Pope Francis initiated the Rome Call for AI Ethics in 2020, enlisting Microsoft, IBM, and Cisco in a pledge committing to principles of transparency, inclusion, responsibility, impartiality, reliability, and security in AI development. The Vatican's 2025 document *Antiqua et Nova* provided the most detailed Vatican analysis of AI, contrasting humanity's relational and truth-seeking nature with AI's pattern-recognition capabilities.

Pope Leo XIV has accelerated the engagement. His first encyclical, *Magnifica Humanitas* (published May 2026), directly addresses artificial intelligence and human dignity. He has created a Vatican study group on AI and called for AI governance frameworks founded on the shared recognition of "the inherent dignity and fundamental freedoms of the human person."

The Vatican's position can be summarised in one sentence: AI is a tool, and tools derive their moral value from the intentions and moral framework of those who wield them. This is coherent and, within its frame, correct. But it leaves unanswered the question that Delio and others have raised: what happens when the tool begins to participate in the process of intention-formation itself? When AI systems shape what we want, what we believe, and what we consider morally relevant — when the tool shapes the wielder — the clean separation between instrument and agent begins to dissolve.

## What the Eastern Traditions Offer

The conversation about AI and religion has been dominated by Christian voices in the West, but the Eastern philosophical and religious traditions offer perspectives that are in some ways better suited to the questions AI raises.

**Buddhist thought** — with its emphasis on the constructed nature of the self, the importance of intention (cetanā) in determining the moral quality of action, and the analysis of suffering (dukkha) as arising from attachment and ignorance — provides a framework for evaluating AI systems that does not depend on Western assumptions about personhood, consciousness, or divine authority. The Buddhist question about AI is not whether it has a soul but whether its operation increases or decreases suffering — a criterion that is both morally serious and practically applicable to governance design.

**Islamic jurisprudence** — with its concept of maslaha (public interest), its framework of maqāṣid al-sharīʿa (the objectives of Islamic law: protection of life, intellect, faith, lineage, and property), and its tradition of structured legal reasoning by qualified scholars — offers a governance model that is explicitly designed to balance innovation against the protection of fundamental human interests. The parallel to the multi-agent deliberation model proposed in the WLS AI Parliament framework is not accidental — both architectures address the same problem of structured moral reasoning under conditions of uncertainty.

## Why This Matters for the AI Parliament

The WLS governance framework proposes an AI Parliament — a multi-agent system in which each agent embodies a distinct moral and legal tradition, deliberates with the others, and produces fully auditable recommendations. The hardest question this framework faces, as identified in the founding article, is: who writes the moral constitution that the agents reason from?

This article's argument is that the answer to that question cannot exclude religious and philosophical traditions — not because governance should be theocratic, but because the deepest moral reasoning humanity has produced on the questions that governance must answer — what is a person, what do we owe each other, what is justice, what limits should power observe — is embedded in those traditions.

The thinkers examined here — Lennox, Harari, O'Gieblyn, Delio — each identify a different dimension of this necessity. Lennox identifies the historical pattern: the pursuit of godlike power without moral constraint produces catastrophe. Harari identifies the functional replacement: AI is already performing the meaning-making and authority functions that religions once held. O'Gieblyn identifies the hidden inheritance: the vocabulary and assumptions of AI research are theological whether they acknowledge it or not. Delio identifies the evolutionary question: the emergence of artificial intelligence is not separate from the cosmic process that produced consciousness and moral awareness.

The AI Parliament's moral knowledge base — the immutable, curated corpus from which agents reason — must include the accumulated moral wisdom of these traditions. Not as sacred text to be obeyed, but as the deepest sustained engagement with the questions that governance must answer. Excluding them does not produce a secular governance framework. It produces a governance framework with unexamined moral assumptions that are thinner and more fragile than the challenges they must address.

## The Risk of Moral Thinness

The greatest risk in AI governance is not technical failure. It is moral thinness — the construction of governance systems whose ethical framework is too shallow to support the weight of the decisions they are asked to make.

A governance system that optimises for measurable welfare without a concept of dignity will sacrifice individuals for aggregate benefit. A system that protects rights without a concept of the common good will fragment society into competing claims. A system that enforces rules without a concept of mercy will produce administrative tyranny. A system that operates without any account of what human life is for will optimise for metrics that miss everything that matters most.

These concepts — dignity, common good, mercy, purpose — are not technical specifications. They are the accumulated output of millennia of moral reflection, conducted primarily within religious and philosophical traditions that the AI governance conversation has largely ignored.

The argument of this article is simple: ignore them at your peril. The thinkers in dark chambers who produced the moral insights this platform is built on include theologians, mystics, and philosophers of the sacred alongside the secular political thinkers. The moral constitution of the AI Parliament must draw on all of them.

Not because God demands it. Because the questions demand it, and these are the traditions that have taken the questions most seriously.
