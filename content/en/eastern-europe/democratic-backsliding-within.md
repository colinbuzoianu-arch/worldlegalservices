# Democratic Backsliding From Within: Hungary, Poland, and Slovakia as a Case Study

*worldlegalservice.com — Eastern European Angle Series, Article 3*

---

Democratic theory spent most of the twentieth century thinking about how democracies die from outside — through military coups, through foreign occupation, through revolutionary violence. The Eastern European experience of the past fifteen years has forced a rethinking of that model. The more consequential threat to consolidated democracies in the twenty-first century is not the tank in the square. It is the parliamentary majority using legitimate democratic procedures to dismantle the institutional constraints that democracy requires to function.

Hungary under Viktor Orbán, Poland under the Law and Justice party, and Slovakia under Robert Fico represent three variations on this theme — three cases in which governments elected through free and fair elections used the democratic process to systematically erode the institutional architecture that gives democracy its substance. Each case is distinct. Together they constitute the most important case study in democratic backsliding available to contemporary governance analysis.

And they happened inside the European Union — which had certified all three countries as consolidated democracies, bound by EU law, subject to EU rule-of-law mechanisms. The EU's response to each case revealed as much about the limits of supranational accountability as about the mechanics of the backsliding itself.

---

## The Hungarian Model

Viktor Orbán's Fidesz party won a two-thirds parliamentary majority in Hungary's 2010 elections — a majority sufficient, under Hungarian constitutional law, to amend the constitution unilaterally. Over the following years, that majority was used with systematic deliberateness to reshape Hungary's institutional architecture.

The constitutional court — which had been an effective check on executive power in Hungary's post-communist transition — was first expanded by packing it with Fidesz loyalists, then had its jurisdiction over budget matters removed, then had its ability to review constitutional amendments curtailed. The effect was to transform the court from an independent check on power into an institution whose independence was nominal.

The judiciary was restructured through the creation of a new administrative court system under executive control and through changes to judicial appointments that gave the governing party decisive influence over the composition of courts at all levels. Judicial independence — the precondition for the rule of law — was systematically compromised through legal means.

The media environment was transformed through a combination of regulatory pressure, advertising market manipulation, and the transfer of media ownership to Fidesz-aligned oligarchs. By 2020, the vast majority of Hungarian media — television, radio, print, and digital — was owned by entities with demonstrated political alignment with the governing party. Independent journalism survived in a compressed and financially precarious space.

Electoral law was amended in ways that consistently advantaged the incumbent: constituency boundaries were redrawn, campaign finance rules were changed, voting arrangements for the Hungarian diaspora — which votes overwhelmingly for Fidesz — were made easier while those for domestic opposition voters in certain demographics were made more complicated.

Each of these changes was implemented through legitimate legal procedures — parliamentary votes, constitutional amendments, regulatory decisions. None required the suspension of democracy. Together they produced what Orbán himself has called an "illiberal democracy" — a system that retains the formal procedures of democratic governance while eliminating the substantive institutional constraints that give those procedures meaning.

## The Polish Variation

Poland's Law and Justice party (PiS) won parliamentary elections in 2015 and immediately began a constitutional crisis that would last until their electoral defeat in 2023.

The specific mechanism in Poland was the judiciary — particularly the Constitutional Tribunal and the Supreme Court. PiS refused to seat judges legitimately appointed by the previous parliament, appointed its own judges to the Tribunal, and passed legislation that gave the parliamentary majority decisive influence over judicial appointments at all levels of the court system.

The European Commission launched infringement proceedings against Poland, eventually referring the matter to the Court of Justice of the EU. The CJEU ruled repeatedly that Polish judicial reforms violated EU law. Poland's government initially refused to comply, creating an unprecedented situation in which an EU member state was openly defying binding judgments of the EU's highest court.

The financial consequences — the suspension of EU cohesion funds contingent on rule-of-law compliance — eventually created sufficient pressure to produce nominal compliance gestures. But the underlying institutional damage was deep.

What the Polish case added to the analytical picture was the stress test of EU enforcement mechanisms. The EU's rule-of-law toolkit — infringement proceedings, Article 7 procedures, conditionality on EU funds — proved slower, more politically constrained, and less effective than the pace of institutional damage required. By the time the mechanisms produced significant consequences, years of damage had been done.

Poland's 2023 election — in which the opposition defeated PiS with the largest voter turnout since 1989 — demonstrated that democratic backsliding can be reversed through electoral means if the opposition can mobilise a sufficiently large counter-mobilisation before the institutional damage becomes irreversible. The subsequent effort to restore judicial independence has illustrated how much harder institutional repair is than institutional damage — changes made by legislation can be reversed by legislation, but changes made to judicial appointments, to institutional culture, and to the informal norms that make formal institutions function cannot be reversed by a single electoral cycle.

## Slovakia's Distinct Path

Slovakia under Robert Fico represents a third variation — one that arrived later and through somewhat different mechanisms, and that has the additional dimension of explicit alignment with Russian foreign policy positions that distinguish it from the Hungarian and Polish cases.

Fico's SMER party governed Slovakia for most of the period between 2006 and 2018, during which time significant concerns about corruption, media freedom, and judicial independence were documented. The 2018 assassination of investigative journalist Ján Kuciak — who was investigating links between Slovak politicians and the Italian mafia — and his fiancée produced mass protests that eventually brought down the government and led to a period of reform-oriented governance.

Fico's return to power in 2023 was followed by steps toward the same institutional playbook used in Hungary: pressuring public media, challenging the independence of prosecutorial institutions, and adopting foreign policy positions that aligned Slovakia with Hungarian Euroscepticism and Russian narrative interests on Ukraine.

Slovakia's case is analytically important because it demonstrates that the backsliding dynamic is not confined to countries with parliamentary supermajorities sufficient for constitutional amendments. It can be pursued through softer means — regulatory pressure, budgetary manipulation, political appointments — that achieve significant institutional damage without the dramatic legal confrontations of the Hungarian and Polish cases.

## What These Cases Reveal About Institutional Design

The Hungary-Poland-Slovakia cases are the most important contemporary evidence for several institutional design conclusions that have direct relevance for the AI governance agenda.

**Constitutional constraints are only as strong as their enforcement mechanism.** Every post-communist constitution was designed with separation of powers, judicial independence, and fundamental rights protection. These provisions failed to prevent backsliding not because they were inadequate as text but because the enforcement mechanisms — courts that could be packed, parliamentary oversight that could be neutralised, EU mechanisms that were too slow — were captured before they could act.

**Supermajority thresholds are insufficient protection when a single party can achieve them.** The two-thirds threshold that allowed Fidesz to amend the Hungarian constitution was designed to require broad consensus for fundamental change. When a single party achieves that threshold through electoral means, the threshold becomes an enabler rather than a constraint. Constitutional design must anticipate the scenario in which a single political actor achieves the formal requirements for fundamental change and build in substantive constraints that survive that scenario.

**Institutional resilience requires redundancy.** The most resilient elements of post-communist governance — civil society, independent journalism, academic institutions, professional associations — survived and eventually contributed to the resistance and reversal of backsliding precisely because they were not dependent on state structures that could be captured. Governance architectures that concentrate all accountability in a single institution, however well-designed, are more vulnerable than those that distribute accountability across multiple overlapping mechanisms.

**Speed asymmetry is the backslider's primary advantage.** Institutional damage can be done faster than institutional accountability mechanisms can respond. Orbán's first-term constitutional transformation was largely completed before EU institutions had developed a coherent response framework. Designing accountability mechanisms that can match the speed of institutional capture is one of the most important challenges in contemporary governance design.

---

## The EU's Institutional Response

The EU's response to democratic backsliding in its member states has been the subject of extensive analysis and near-universal criticism — from those who believe the response was too slow and too weak, and from those who believe supranational intervention in member state governance crosses lines of subsidiarity and sovereignty that should be respected.

The criticism from those who believe the response was inadequate is better supported by the evidence. The Article 7 procedure — the EU's primary mechanism for responding to serious rule-of-law violations — requires unanimity in the Council, giving any single member state veto power. Hungary and Poland protected each other from the Article 7 consequences that each might otherwise have faced.

The infringement proceedings, while ultimately producing some compliance, moved on timescales measured in years while institutional damage was inflicted on timescales measured in months. The rule-of-law conditionality mechanism — suspending EU funds for member states in serious rule-of-law violation — was adopted in 2020 and only began to produce significant financial consequences after the damage in both Hungary and Poland was already severe.

The lesson for supranational governance design is the same as the lesson for domestic governance design: accountability mechanisms must be designed with adversarial conditions in mind, must be capable of operating at the speed of the threat, and must not depend on the cooperation of the actors whose behaviour they are designed to constrain.
