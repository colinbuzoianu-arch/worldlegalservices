# The Accession Lever: EU Enlargement as Governance Reform Engine — and Its Limits

*worldlegalservices.com — Eastern European Angle Series, Article 5*

---

The European Union's eastern enlargement — the admission of ten new member states in 2004, followed by Romania and Bulgaria in 2007 and Croatia in 2013 — is the most successful large-scale governance reform program in modern history. Twenty countries that had been communist states a decade or fifteen years earlier were transformed into functioning market democracies with independent judiciaries, protected civil liberties, and institutional frameworks broadly compatible with those of Western European democracies.

This achievement is routinely underappreciated, partly because the subsequent democratic backsliding in some new member states has made the narrative of enlargement success more complicated, and partly because the transformation happened gradually enough to seem inevitable in retrospect. It was not inevitable. It required the sustained application of a governance reform mechanism — the EU accession process — that has no parallel in its combination of scope, depth, and conditionality.

Understanding how the accession lever worked, where it worked imperfectly, and why its effectiveness declined after accession is essential to understanding both the current situation in Eastern Europe and the design requirements for governance reform mechanisms that are built to last rather than built to achieve accession.

---

## How the Accession Lever Worked

The EU accession process required candidate countries to adopt and implement the *acquis communautaire* — the accumulated body of EU law, now running to tens of thousands of legal acts — as a condition of membership. This requirement extended far beyond trade and economic regulation. It encompassed judicial systems, anti-corruption frameworks, civil service reform, media regulation, environmental standards, labour law, and fundamental rights protection.

The mechanism that made this requirement effective was the combination of credible conditionality and substantial material incentive. Access to the EU's single market — the world's largest — was a transformative economic prize. EU structural and cohesion funds — which for the poorer candidate countries represented multiples of their domestic public investment capacity — were available only to members in good standing. The conditionality was credible because the EU had demonstrated willingness to delay and complicate accession for countries that failed to meet the requirements.

This combination produced something that purely domestic reform processes rarely achieve: a reform imperative that was insulated from the immediate incentives of incumbent political actors. A government that wanted to slow anti-corruption reform for domestic political reasons faced the external constraint that doing so would jeopardise EU accession — a prize that commanded sufficient popular support to make obstruction politically costly.

The EU essentially created an external accountability mechanism for governance reform — one that operated with greater consistency and credibility than the domestic accountability mechanisms that the reform was supposed to strengthen.

## The Moldova and Georgia Cases

The accession lever is currently being applied, with variable results, to Ukraine, Moldova, and Georgia — the three countries granted EU candidate status in 2022-2023.

Moldova's accession process has proceeded with relative smoothness under President Maia Sandu's pro-European government, which has used the accession framework to pursue significant anti-corruption and judicial reform. Moldova's transformation is genuinely impressive given its starting conditions — one of Europe's poorest countries, with significant corruption, a frozen conflict in Transnistria, and intense Russian pressure. The accession lever is working in Moldova in approximately the way it worked for Central European candidates in the late 1990s.

Georgia's trajectory has diverged sharply. The Georgian Dream government's adoption of Russian-style "foreign agents" legislation, the disputed 2024 elections, and the government's suspension of EU accession negotiations represent a case where the accession lever has failed — where a government has calculated that maintaining power through democratic backsliding is preferable to the governance reforms that EU accession requires. The Georgian case illustrates the accession lever's fundamental limitation: it works only when the government applying for membership genuinely wants to join, and when that desire is politically sustainable against domestic authoritarian pressures.

Ukraine's accession process is complicated by the obvious fact that it is taking place during an active war. The reform requirements are real and are being pursued — the Rule of Law Roadmap adopted by Ukraine in 2025 represents a serious reform commitment — but the institutional capacity to implement them, the political economy of reform during wartime, and the timeline of accession negotiations create tensions that have no precedent in previous enlargement processes.

## The Western Balkans: Enlargement Fatigue and Its Consequences

The Western Balkans — Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, North Macedonia, Albania, Montenegro, and Kosovo — have been in various stages of EU accession negotiations for two decades, with limited progress toward actual membership.

The enlargement fatigue that set in among EU member states after the 2004-2007 enlargements, combined with the genuine governance challenges in Western Balkans candidates, has produced a situation in which the accession lever has lost much of its effectiveness. Candidate countries that have been in the process for twenty years without credible prospects of membership in the foreseeable future have diminishing incentives to accept the political costs of reform. Politicians who make anti-corruption commitments under EU pressure face domestic constituencies that have become sceptical of a process that seems to reward compliance with delay rather than membership.

Serbia's case is particularly instructive. Serbia has been an official EU candidate since 2012, has formally opened and closed various negotiating chapters, and has simultaneously maintained close relationships with Russia and China that are in tension with EU foreign policy alignment. The EU accession process has not been able to produce the kind of irreversible reform trajectory it produced in Central European candidates, partly because the credibility of the membership offer has been consistently undermined by enlargement scepticism among existing member states.

The consequence — a Western Balkans that is neither firmly anchored in the EU nor firmly anchored elsewhere, characterised by governance systems that have partially reformed without fully consolidating — is a source of ongoing regional instability. The governance vacuum that EU accession was supposed to fill has been partly occupied by Chinese investment, Russian political influence, and Gulf state engagement, none of which comes with governance conditionality.

---

## Why the Lever Weakens After Accession

The most important limitation of the accession lever is not its effectiveness before accession — which, as the evidence shows, was substantial. It is its near-total ineffectiveness after accession.

Once a country is an EU member, the conditionality that drove reform disappears. The material incentive structure inverts: structural funds flow to member states regardless of governance performance, EU budget contributions are fixed by treaty, and market access is guaranteed by membership. The accountability mechanism that operated through the threat of delayed or denied accession has no post-accession equivalent.

The EU's rule-of-law mechanisms — infringement proceedings, Article 7, the rule-of-law conditionality regulation — are slower, more politically constrained, and less effective than the accession process. They were designed as crisis responses rather than ongoing governance accountability mechanisms. The Hungarian case demonstrated their limitations over a decade of democratic backsliding.

This post-accession accountability gap is the most important structural design failure of the EU's governance reform program. It produced exactly the outcome that institutional design theory predicts: when the enforcement mechanism disappears, the reforms that were adopted in response to it begin to erode.

---

## Designing for Durability

The lesson of the accession lever for governance reform design is not that external conditionality does not work — it clearly does, within its scope and timeframe. The lesson is that governance reform driven primarily by external conditionality does not automatically produce the institutional culture, the civil society capacity, and the domestic accountability mechanisms that make reform durable after the external pressure is removed.

The countries that maintained their democratic progress after accession — the Baltic states, Poland until 2015, Czech Republic, Slovenia — were those where the accession process coincided with and reinforced domestic reform dynamics: strong civil society, independent media, professional judicial cultures, and political traditions that gave domestic accountability mechanisms enough strength to operate without continuous external reinforcement.

The countries where post-accession backsliding occurred — Hungary, Poland under PiS, Romania's persistent governance challenges — were those where the accession process had reformed formal institutions without adequately building the informal institutional culture that gives formal institutions their substance.

The design implication for the WLS governance framework is direct: governance reform mechanisms must be designed to build institutional culture, not merely institutional form. The AI Parliament's mandatory renewal requirements, the learning platform's continuous engagement model, the token system's anti-accumulation architecture — these are all mechanisms for sustaining institutional culture over time, not merely for establishing institutional form at a single moment of founding.
