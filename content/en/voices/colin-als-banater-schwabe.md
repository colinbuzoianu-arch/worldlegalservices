# Colin as a Banat Swabian

It began with a voice. My mother's voice.

She was half Banat Swabian, and she had decided for me before I could decide anything for myself: I was to speak German. Not someday, not as a foreign language at school, but from the very beginning, as part of my air, my breath. Even though our family lived in Romania, even though the streets around us spoke Romanian and the neighbours spoke Romanian and the television spoke Romanian — in our home, there was also this other language. My mother taught it to me like an heirloom you don't keep behind glass but take into your hands every day, until it feels like part of your own body. And so it has remained. German was never a language I learned. It was a language in which I became.

My whole life revolves around Timișoara, my hometown, and around a question that resonates in this city: how does one actually come to be called a Swabian in the Banat? The answer lies centuries in the past. During the reign of Maria Theresa, Swabians from Alsace-Lorraine were settled across various regions of the Habsburg Monarchy — among them the Banat. They came not as conquerors but as builders. They contributed to the infrastructure, dug canals, constructed roads and houses. The Bega Canal, which still flows through Timișoara today, bears their handwriting. And when you walk through the central neighbourhoods now, beautifully restored, you can admire the Habsburg architecture — those facades that speak of a time when Timișoara was a crossroads of European cultures. Within these walls lies a piece of my own history.

I graduated from the German Nikolaus Lenau Lyceum in Timișoara in 1997, specialising in Literature and Language. I remember the early years there precisely: when I started, the Banat Swabians still formed a majority in the classrooms. You could hear German in the corridors, during breaks, in the schoolyard. It was simply a given. But with each passing school year, the classes grew thinner. Families emigrated, one after another, towards Germany. It was a quiet disappearing — not a dramatic flight but a steady departure, one you only noticed when you looked around and the seats were empty. The language remained in the classrooms, but the people who had brought it from home were gone.

During that time I had my first encounters with the German-speaking world beyond the textbooks. Three times I spent a month each as an exchange student in Gratkorn, near Graz, in Austria. For three months I was able to attend school in Saarbrücken. For a teenager from Timișoara, these were not trips — they were expeditions into a world I had known only through the language my mother had given me. Suddenly that language had streets and supermarkets and bus timetables. It had an everyday life. And I realised that in that everyday life, I was not a stranger. The language carried me.

After school I began studying at the German acting department of the Academy of Music in Timișoara. My father had been an actor — a leading man in many productions at the National Theatre of Timișoara. He himself did not speak German, but the stage knows no language borders. From him I inherited the love of theatre, that feeling that a room can transform when someone fills it with words and presence. Already in my first year of study I was able to join the German State Theatre in Timișoara. From 1998 I played my first roles, and I stayed there until 2009 — eleven years on a stage where German was the language of art.

During those years I had the opportunity to perform several times on tours in Germany. I completed a scholarship at the University of Music and Theatre Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy in Leipzig. And after every tour I had the same impression: as though I had visited another world. Western Europe looked so much more polished than Romania at the time — so much more effortless in its prosperity. On returning, the grey communist buildings greeted me every time, pulling me back into my reality. Into my life. Into my city. Into my family. It was not a bitter moment, but a sober one. And it was precisely in this period that most of my Lenau classmates emigrated with their families.

My parents did not want to leave Romania. My father did not speak German — his world was the Romanian theatre. My mother was employed. So we stayed. And I stayed with them.

What I did observe on my travels, however, was something that occupies me to this day: Romania was seen in the West as part of the third world. Hardly anyone knew where it lay on the map. And the Roma, whose nomadic origins and history almost no one understood, were equated with the Romanian people. Through the thefts and offences of individuals, an image was formed that overshadowed an entire country. From a historical perspective I felt this to be profoundly unjust — because Romania had, over centuries, held back attacks from the Turks, Tatars, and other peoples, ultimately also to the benefit of a Europe that was able to develop in peace. On a visit to the Danube Swabian Cultural Foundation I admired the traditional costumes of the old Swabians, traced the history a little further — and it was the only moment on those journeys when I felt that someone was making this shared history between cultures visible.

## My Grandfather

I want to write here also about my grandfather, because his story belongs to mine.

He was recruited into the Waffen-SS during the Second World War — not out of conviction, but because he was recruited, like so many young men from the Banat. He served at various theatres of war. Last, he fought in the final battle of Stalingrad and was taken prisoner. Seven years he spent in captivity in Odessa. Seven years. Then he returned to Lugoj, to his family. Before the war he had been a fireman. After the war he lived on as a carpenter. He spoke little of what he had experienced. From the war he brought home only one thing: an iron cross bearing the engraved words: "Thy will be done." I still have that cross in my house today. Sometimes I hold it in my hand and think about what a human being can endure and still return — to the simple things, to the wood, to the work, to the family.

## Between Stages

The times changed. After my years at the German State Theatre I moved to the Romanian National Theatre. I performed a great deal, I resolved to believe in Romania's future. The idea of emigrating — as most of my former Lenau classmates had done — I set aside deliberately. Romania was my home. I wanted to make it one.

But then my father died. My mother had been ill since I was fourteen. And although I performed in most of the National Theatre's productions, I could barely make it financially from one month to the next. So I gave up the theatre. It was not a loud farewell. It was a quiet leaving, a ceasing that felt like the end of a long sentence missing its full stop.

And once again, the German language caught me as I fell.

Every job in IT that followed had to do with German. My excellent command of the language opened doors that would otherwise have remained closed. I worked in support, in domain administration, in client and service management — I tried everything. And over time, through daily conversations with technicians from the most diverse fields, I began to understand the IT world on a deeper technical level. I was not a computer scientist. I was an actor who spoke German and knew how to listen. And that was enough to understand.

## Two Sons, One Language

For six years now I have been working in occupational health and safety. The services I manage are for German and international clients. But the inflation, Romania's economic situation — one of the worst in Europe at this moment — all of it makes me think once again of departure. My parents are no longer here. What holds me is no longer duty but habit. And habit is no reason to stay.

I have two sons. Ari, my elder, does not live with us — but he lives inside me, in every thought about the future I form. The fact that he does not grow up under my roof changes nothing about the place he holds in my heart, the same place as everything that matters most to me. And Hugo, my four-year-old — I am teaching him German, just as my mother did with me. The same decision, the same gesture across generations: passing on a language like a promise you do not want to break. I want to offer them both the chance to study and work in this language, if they choose to. Hugo is also a quarter German — for my wife is herself a quarter German, her maternal grandfather too was a Banat Swabian. So this origin flows into him from both sides, quietly, like an underground current. And Ari carries it forward in his own way, whether he knows it yet or not.

## On the Way

And yet I am hard to categorise. A Romanian IT professional with a training as an actor. An outsider with an unusual life story, who nonetheless speaks German as though it were his mother tongue — because in a way, it is. In job interviews I am often at a disadvantage, if only because of the distance. My life in Romania gives the impression that I abandoned the German side of my identity long ago — as though I had decided not to belong there. But that is not true. I have spent most of my working life in this language. I have stood on stage in this language, laughed, wept, fallen silent. I have solved technical problems and advised clients in this language. German is not an appendix to my CV. It is the foundation.

I want to emigrate. I am disillusioned with Romania's current situation, with a political class I cannot identify with, with the inertia of a people whose mentality I no longer feel as my own. Perhaps the voices of my German education, of my youth in Gratkorn and Saarbrücken, of my years on the stage of the German State Theatre, now speak louder than everything that defines me as Romanian. I am caught between two mentalities that are diametrically opposed.

But perhaps "caught" is the wrong word. Perhaps I am not caught, but on my way. And the answer that makes sense to me is simple: to finally live somewhere in the DACH region. With my family. With the possibility of continuing to discover this side of my origin. And to offer my children — both of them — a future in which they do not have to choose between two worlds, but may inhabit them both.
