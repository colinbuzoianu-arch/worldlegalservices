# Gratkorn

My time in Gratkorn was my first encounter with Austria — and, indeed, with any country other than Romania. For three years I was invited as a guest pupil, through a Red Cross project, to a family in Austria. The family was called Preitler, and every year I spent about a month in their house.

I was in the sixth grade when, in the first year, I came to Gratkorn to stay with the Preitler family. The journey was by train. I remember that leaving Romania was itself the greatest change. After an all but endless wait at the border, during which the train was still being searched for refugees, Hungary already looked entirely different. In one year — I remember it precisely — the customs officers found a fugitive hidden in the ceiling of the carriage corridor. That stretched the wait at the Romanian-Hungarian border even longer.

The next major stop was Budapest. There we — the pupils travelling with the Red Cross to Austria — leaned out of the open windows of the train and looked around. The smell of the train, of the station, the light breeze: for me it was a moment in which the adventure of not being with my parents, alone in a foreign country, conjured a broad smile onto my face. That feeling has always stayed with me. I recall it on every journey to Austria or Germany — whether on the train, or in a coach on the motorway, in which I could never sleep and always sat beside the driver, out of sheer excitement for the time ahead.

In Austria it was Gratkorn, near Graz, where I stayed with the Preitler family. I met Mr Preitler at the station. He had white hair and wore a white shirt, and he collected me in a red Toyota Corolla. For the car enthusiast I was back then, a Toyota was something extraordinary, wonderful, never before encountered. In post-revolutionary Romania there were still mainly Dacias and local cars at that time, most of them in poor condition. The drive from the station to the Preitlers' house, in a red Toyota Corolla whose engine purred evenly and harmoniously, was already more than I could ever have imagined. Even the grass was greener in Austria — or so it seemed to me. The landscape, with its widely spaced houses and its cultivated fields, was a contrast to Romania's untilled fields, as most were after the Revolution, and to the dust spread everywhere, which lay on cars, on buildings, even on the fields, and was part of our everyday visual world.

From the Preitler family's house I still keep the memory of a stream at the end of their back garden, where I often played; of a large chest freezer, of a kind we did not have at home back then; and above all of the fact that the house itself was single-storey, which in Timișoara was, at the very least, unusual. Where we lived there were either apartment blocks or houses without upper floors. That has changed a great deal since then in the architecture of Timișoara. Timișoara itself, as a city, is barely recognisable when I try to call the post-revolutionary years back to mind.

I met Mrs Preitler in a blue-and-white dress, her hair tied back, glasses on her nose. She radiated calm and humour, and I liked her from the very first second. I was glad to be in their house — and yet I also felt a little foreign. I remember that towards the end of the month the homesickness grew ever stronger. Not because anything was missing there; I took in everything I saw and learned — the different culture, indeed the different way of thinking, the landscape and the nature. But I was still a child, and at some point I simply wanted to go back to my parents.

One memory of my time there has remained especially vivid. I was given a bicycle, on which I was allowed to move about the area. It was a half-racer, and I loved riding around on it. One particular route imprinted itself deeply on me, because on part of it, on the way back — that is, downhill in this case — I built up speed, and my attention thereby held on to every detail. It was a road leading out of Gratkorn, through a forest, crossing a stretch of highway, and at some point everything grew ever quieter, darker, deeper in the woods; the air grew fresher, and the road itself ever fuller of leaves. My rides along this path always ended at a small chapel in the forest, with the Holy Mary, and sometimes a candle was burning inside. I spent time there in quiet, in my thoughts, in prayers, and in a deep admiration of the forest, of nature, of the wind rushing through the leaves.

My return journey to Romania was often bound up with sorrow and joy at once — especially when I saw Arad, the first Romanian city after the border, with its grey buildings, with the dust that lay on everything, with the people likewise dressed in extinguished colours, wandering the streets with grave, almost sorrowful faces. I felt grateful to have experienced another world, and I stepped down from the train sobered, to embrace my father tightly on the platform.
