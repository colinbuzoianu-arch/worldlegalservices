# We Have Laws for Everything Except the Machines That Will Govern Us

*worldlegalservice.com — AI Governance Series, Article 2*

---

There is a law governing the maximum curvature of a banana sold in the European Union. There are regulations specifying the minimum fat content of products allowed to be called cream. There are directives on the acoustic emissions of lawn equipment, on the recyclability of packaging for cosmetic products, on the working hours of junior doctors, on the labeling of honey by floral origin.

The European Union alone has produced over one hundred thousand pages of binding legal text. The accumulated legislation of modern nation states covers, in aggregate, nearly every conceivable human activity.

And yet there is no serious legal framework governing what happens when an artificial intelligence system influences a public policy decision that affects millions of people.

Not a gap. Not an oversight waiting to be filled at the next legislative session. A vacuum — systematic, consequential, and growing faster than any legislature is moving to close it.

---

## What the EU AI Act Actually Does

The EU AI Act, which entered into force in 2024, is the most ambitious attempt so far to regulate artificial intelligence at a jurisdictional scale. It deserves credit for existing — for establishing the principle that AI systems operating within European markets are subject to legal obligations, not merely market forces.

But read it carefully and a structural limitation becomes clear. The Act was designed to regulate AI as a **product** — a system that humans use as a tool to assist in making decisions. It classifies systems by risk level, imposes transparency and documentation requirements on high-risk applications, prohibits certain uses outright, and creates accountability obligations for developers and deployers.

What it does not address — what it was not designed to address — is AI as a **participant** in governance itself. The scenario where an AI system is not merely a tool that a minister consults, but an active component of the deliberative process through which public policy is shaped. The multi-agent architecture described in this series. The algorithmic accountability layer proposed in Article 1. The broader question of what legal status attaches to a system whose outputs are not recommendations in any ordinary sense but structural inputs into how public resources are allocated and how public power is exercised.

The Act's framework of developers, deployers, and users breaks down entirely when the system in question is not a product being sold into a market but an institutional component of governance itself.

---

## The Three Legal Vacuums

Examined carefully, the absence of a legal framework for AI in governance resolves into three distinct and serious vacuums.

**The first is liability.** When an AI system contributes to a governance decision that causes harm — a policy recommendation that proves catastrophically wrong, an algorithmic assessment that systematically discriminates, a resource allocation model that produces outcomes nobody intended — who is responsible?

Current legal doctrine offers no clean answer. Product liability law was designed for physical goods. Professional liability was designed for individual practitioners. Administrative law governs the decisions of human officials. None of these frameworks maps cleanly onto a situation where the causal chain runs through a system that is neither a product in the ordinary sense, nor a professional, nor an official.

The practical consequence is that AI-influenced governance decisions currently exist in a liability vacuum — a space where serious harm can occur, causation can be traced to an algorithmic system, and no legal mechanism exists to translate that causation into responsibility.

**The second is constitutional status.** Democratic constitutions were written to govern the exercise of public power by human institutions and human officials. They specify who may make which decisions, subject to what procedural requirements, with what rights of challenge and review available to affected citizens.

None of this was designed with AI participation in mind. When an AI system influences a public decision — even as an advisory input — questions of constitutional law arise that existing doctrine cannot answer. Is the decision-maker's reliance on algorithmic output consistent with their constitutional obligation to exercise personal judgment? Does a citizen's right to challenge an administrative decision extend to challenging the algorithmic inputs that shaped it? What procedural requirements attach to the use of AI in public deliberation?

These questions will eventually be answered by courts, which is the worst possible way for them to be answered — case by case, retrospectively, after harm has occurred, without the systematic thinking that legislative design allows.

**The third is jurisdictional.** AI governance systems, if they are to be meaningful, cannot operate within the boundaries of a single nation state. The problems they are designed to address — resource misallocation, accountability failure, policy incoherence — are transnational. The knowledge bases they reason from are global. The standards of accountability they enforce should not vary depending on which side of a border a citizen happens to live on.

No international legal instrument currently exists that could govern a transnational AI governance architecture. The frameworks that exist — the UN Charter, international humanitarian law, trade agreements, bilateral treaties — were all designed for a world of human actors and human institutions. Extending them to cover AI participants in governance requires legal innovation at a scale that international institutions have not yet seriously contemplated.

## Why Legislatures Are Moving Slowly

The legislative response to AI has been characterized, almost universally, by a lag between technological development and legal response. This is partly inevitable — law has always followed technology rather than anticipated it — but in this case the lag is unusually dangerous, and its causes are worth understanding.

**Technical complexity creates learned helplessness.** Legislators who do not understand how large language models work, how multi-agent systems deliberate, or what an objective function is, tend to defer to technical experts — who are usually employed by the companies whose systems need regulating. The result is legislation that reflects industry preferences more than public interest, dressed in technical language that obscures the value choices embedded in the regulatory design.

**The beneficiaries of the vacuum are organized; the victims are not.** The technology companies building AI systems have every incentive to delay serious regulation — every year of the vacuum is a year in which they build market position, establish technical standards, and create facts on the ground that future regulation will have to accommodate. The citizens who will eventually be subject to AI-influenced governance decisions have no comparable organization, no comparable resources, and often no awareness that the design choices being made now will constrain their rights for decades.

**The political imagination required is genuinely difficult.** Writing good law for AI governance requires the ability to reason about systems that do not yet exist at scale, in institutional contexts that have no historical precedent, producing consequences that are genuinely uncertain. This is hard. It is easier to regulate the curvature of bananas — the problem is concrete, the stakeholders are identifiable, the consequences of getting it wrong are bounded.

The difficulty is not an excuse. It is a reason to start earlier and think harder.

---

## What the Legal Framework Needs to Contain

A serious legal architecture for AI in governance does not need to be completed before any system is deployed. But it needs to exist as a framework — a set of principles and structures that deployment decisions are required to operate within — before the vacuum is filled by accident rather than design.

At minimum, that framework needs to address five questions.

**Institutional independence.** Any AI system involved in governance must be structurally independent from the institutions it serves and evaluates. This means legal insulation equivalent to — or stronger than — the protections afforded to central banks and constitutional courts. Independence cannot be a policy preference; it must be a legal requirement with enforcement mechanisms.

**Transparency as a right.** Citizens affected by decisions that involved AI input must have a legally enforceable right to understand how that input was generated. Not a summary. Not a press release. Access to the methodology, the data, and the reasoning chain — subject to legitimate limitations for security and privacy, but with those limitations themselves subject to independent review.

**Mandatory pre-deployment assessment.** Before any AI system is deployed in a governance role, an independent legal and technical assessment must certify that the liability, constitutional, and jurisdictional questions have been answered — not resolved perfectly, but answered with sufficient rigor that deployment is proceeding on a known legal foundation rather than into a vacuum.

**International coordination mechanisms.** The transnational nature of AI governance requires new international legal instruments — not merely guidelines or recommendations, but binding frameworks with dispute resolution mechanisms and enforcement capacity. This is a generational legislative project. It will not be completed quickly. That is precisely why it needs to begin now.

**Sunset and review requirements.** Given the pace of technological change, any legal framework for AI governance must include mandatory review cycles — legal provisions that require the framework to be reassessed against actual deployment experience at defined intervals, with a presumption of revision rather than entrenchment.

---

## The Cost of Waiting

There is a tempting logic that says: wait until the technology matures, wait until we understand what we are actually dealing with, then write the law. This logic has governed most legislative responses to AI so far, and it is producing exactly the outcome it deserves.

While legislatures wait, systems are being built. Standards are being set — not by democratic deliberation, but by engineering decisions made inside companies operating without legal constraint. The architecture of AI governance, when it arrives, will reflect the values embedded in those early systems. The legal framework that eventually emerges will have to accommodate the facts already created.

The banana curvature regulation exists because there was a constituency that wanted it, a legislative process that produced it, and an enforcement mechanism that makes it binding. It is, in its small way, an example of governance working as designed.

The machines that will shape how public power is exercised in the coming decades deserve at least as much serious legal attention as the curvature of fruit.

We have not yet begun to give it to them.
