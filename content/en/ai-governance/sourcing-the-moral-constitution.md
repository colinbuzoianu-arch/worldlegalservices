# Sourcing the Moral Constitution: Criteria for the AI Governance Corpus

*worldlegalservices.com — AI Governance Series*

---

The AI Parliament article ends with a question it does not resolve: **who writes the moral constitution?**

Every obvious answer reproduces the problem the architecture was designed to solve. A government-curated corpus encodes the values of whoever holds power. A corporate-curated corpus encodes the values of whoever holds capital. An academic-curated corpus encodes the blind spots of credentialed thinkers operating inside their own incentive structures. A democratically-curated corpus — selected by majority vote or stakeholder consultation — encodes whatever majorities currently prefer, which is precisely the moving target a constitution is supposed to anchor against.

This article proposes an answer. Not a complete one — the work of building the corpus is generational, and no single article can specify the full methodology. But a coherent first principle, with concrete criteria that follow from it, and a set of historical exemplars that demonstrate what the filter actually selects for.

The proposal can be stated in one sentence:

**The moral constitution should be sourced primarily from thinkers whose work survived without institutional incentive — who wrote in obscurity, exile, imprisonment, or self-imposed marginality, holding nothing but the belief that what they were saying was true.**

What follows is the case for that principle, the criteria it generates, and the methodology it implies.

---

## The Epistemological Argument

This is not a romantic position. It is an epistemological claim.

Ideas produced inside incentive structures that reward particular conclusions are statistically more likely to reflect those incentives than to reflect anything about the underlying moral reality. This is not a controversial claim in any other domain. We routinely discount the testimony of expert witnesses paid by one party in litigation. We discount the conclusions of industry-funded studies. We discount political speeches written to win elections. We do this because we understand that the absence of incentive distortion is a precondition for the inference from *X claimed Y* to *Y is probably true*.

The same logic applies to moral philosophy.

When a person writes from a position where saying the truth costs them money, status, comfort, or — in some cases — their life, and they say it anyway, the inference structure changes. Their willingness to bear those costs is itself evidence that they believed what they were saying. Their work is not necessarily correct — many obscure thinkers were also wrong — but the bias profile of their work is fundamentally different from the bias profile of work produced inside structures of reward.

This is a filter, not a guarantee. The filter does not select for truth. It selects for *the absence of corrupting incentive*. The next layer of work — scholarly, intercultural, time-tested — selects for what within that filtered corpus has survived contact with reality.

## Seven Exemplars

The principle is best understood through the figures who embody it. Seven thinkers, each from a different tradition, each demonstrating a different mechanism by which the filter operates.

### Spinoza (1632–1677)

Excommunicated from the Sephardic Jewish community of Amsterdam in 1656 — at age twenty-three — under the harshest *cherem* the community ever issued. The text of the excommunication forbids any contact with him, including reading his writings. He spent the remaining twenty-one years of his life grinding optical lenses in a back room in The Hague, declining a professorship at Heidelberg because it would have required him to constrain what he taught. He published most of his work anonymously or posthumously. His *Ethics* — written in geometric proof form, attempting to derive all moral conclusions from definitions and axioms — was published the year of his death, knowing he would not be present to defend it.

Three centuries later, the *Ethics* remains one of the most studied works in Western philosophy. Spinoza had no incentive to write it. He had every incentive not to.

### Simone Weil (1909–1943)

Born into a comfortable French-Jewish intellectual family. Refused that comfort on principle from her early twenties. Took a year of leave from teaching to work in Renault and Alsthom factories so she could write about industrial labor from the inside of it. Volunteered in the Spanish Civil War. After fleeing France, she insisted on eating only the rations available to those in the occupied zone, refusing the food her doctors prescribed for her tuberculosis. She died in a sanatorium in Kent at thirty-four, almost certainly of self-imposed starvation.

Her writings on affliction, justice, attention, and rooted human existence were almost entirely published after her death. She wrote with the knowledge that her work would have no audience in her lifetime. The work, when it finally circulated, became one of the most influential moral philosophies of the twentieth century.

### Alexander Solzhenitsyn (1918–2008)

Imprisoned in the Soviet Gulag for eight years after writing a letter that contained mild criticism of Stalin. Continued writing inside the camps, memorizing thousands of lines of verse because writing them down would mean execution. After release, he composed *The Gulag Archipelago* in secret across multiple safe houses, distributing manuscript sections to different keepers so that no single arrest could destroy the work. When the KGB seized one copy in 1973 and his typist was interrogated to death, he authorized publication in the West knowing it would mean exile or imprisonment.

He wrote in conditions where the act of writing was itself a capital offense. The work survived because he wrote it anyway.

### Ibn Rushd / Averroes (1126–1198)

Andalusian polymath — physician, jurist, philosopher — produced the most influential commentaries on Aristotle of the medieval period. Lived in the increasingly contracting Islamic west, where philosophical inquiry was tolerated by some caliphs and suppressed by others. In 1195, the Almohad caliph Abu Yusuf Yaqub al-Mansur — under pressure from religious authorities — banned his books, ordered them burned, and exiled him to Lucena. He died two years later in Marrakesh, his work largely destroyed in the Arabic-speaking world.

His commentaries survived because they were translated into Hebrew and Latin in the margins of empire — preserved by Jewish scholars in Andalusia and Christian scholars in Toledo. Without Ibn Rushd, the Aristotelian foundations of Aquinas, of scholastic legal reasoning, of the entire architecture of European university jurisprudence, are not possible. He wrote knowing that his own civilization was closing around him. The work outlived the closure.

### Boethius (c. 477–524)

Former Roman consul and senior minister at the Ostrogothic court of Theodoric. Accused of treason — almost certainly falsely, in a political purge — and imprisoned in Pavia. While awaiting execution, he wrote *The Consolation of Philosophy*, a dialogue between himself and Philosophy personified, addressing fortune, providence, the nature of the good, and how a just person should respond to unjust punishment. He was tortured and executed.

The *Consolation* was one of the most widely read texts of the entire Middle Ages — translated by King Alfred into Old English, by Chaucer into Middle English, by Elizabeth I into Early Modern English. He wrote it for no audience. He could not have known anyone would read it.

### Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855)

Danish philosopher who spent his entire career outside institutional academia, publishing under pseudonyms — Johannes de Silentio, Anti-Climacus, Constantin Constantius, Vigilius Haufniensis — to force readers to confront the ideas without the authority of the author. Funded his publications himself, exhausting his paternal inheritance in the process. In his final years, attacked the established Danish Lutheran church directly through a one-man journal called *The Moment*, becoming a public laughingstock — caricatured weekly in the satirical paper *Corsaren*. Collapsed in the street at forty-two, financially ruined and physically broken.

He founded existentialism without intending to found anything. The institutional philosophical tradition he opposed adopted him a century later.

### Emil Cioran (1911–1995)

Romanian-born French philosopher. Refused every academic position offered to him. Refused every major literary prize, including the Roger Caillois, the Sainte-Beuve, and the Paul Morand — returning prize money each time. Lived his entire adult life in a sixth-floor garret on rue de l'Odéon in Paris under stateless status after 1947. At thirty-six, he abandoned Romanian — the language in which he was already established as a major writer — and chose to write in French, in which he was nobody. The choice doubled his obscurity: he gave up the language that recognized him and adopted one that did not.

He survived on freelance editing, occasional translation work, and the support of his lifelong partner Simone Boué. His aphoristic books — *The Trouble with Being Born*, *The Temptation to Exist*, *A Short History of Decay* — are now translated into more than thirty languages.

## The Pattern

These seven figures do not share a single tradition. They span Sephardic Judaism, secular humanism, Russian Orthodox Christianity, Islamic Aristotelianism, late Roman Christianity, Lutheran existentialism, and post-religious despair. They span thirteen centuries. They wrote in Latin, Hebrew, Arabic, Russian, Danish, French, German.

What they share is structural, not doctrinal.

**First: each refused — actively, not accidentally — a structure that would have rewarded them for saying something other than what they believed.** Spinoza turned down Heidelberg. Weil refused her family's comfort and her doctors' prescriptions. Solzhenitsyn refused silence inside the Gulag. Ibn Rushd refused to soften his Aristotelianism for the religious authorities who controlled his political survival. Boethius refused to confess to a treason he had not committed. Kierkegaard refused the academic legitimacy that would have come with abandoning his pseudonymity. Cioran refused every form of recognition his work earned him.

The refusal is the signal. It is what distinguishes these figures from contemporaries with comparable talent who took the available rewards and were forgotten.

**Second: each produced work that survived the conditions of its production.** This is the second filter. Many thinkers refused incentive structures and produced work that did not survive — because the work was not actually good, because no one preserved it, because the historical accident did not occur. Survival across centuries, across translations, across traditions, is itself evidence of something.

Not proof. Evidence.

**Third: each was eventually validated by traditions that had no reason to validate them.** Spinoza was reviled in his lifetime and is now taught in every philosophy department from Buenos Aires to Beijing. Weil was an obscure factory worker whose notebooks now form syllabi at the Sorbonne and Harvard. Solzhenitsyn's *Gulag Archipelago* was read into the Soviet record by Gorbachev's reformers. Ibn Rushd's commentaries shaped Catholic scholasticism. Boethius defined medieval Christian moral philosophy from a pagan-influenced Roman pen. Kierkegaard's existentialism was claimed by atheists and Christians alike. Cioran is read across political and religious lines that he himself would have refused.

The pattern of cross-tradition validation is the third filter. It is what distinguishes durable moral insight from tradition-specific advocacy that happens to have survived.

## The Criteria That Follow

From the pattern, a set of criteria emerges. These are the criteria a curatorial body would apply to candidate works for inclusion in the AI governance moral corpus.

**Criterion 1 — Absence of corrupting incentive at the time of writing.** Did the author write under conditions that rewarded them for the conclusions they reached? Court philosophers, propagandists of any regime, paid theologians, industry-funded ethicists, polling-driven politicians — all fail this filter. This is not a moral judgment of the individuals. It is a statistical claim about the inference value of their work for our specific purpose.

**Criterion 2 — Survival across centuries without institutional sponsorship.** Did the work survive because institutions promoted it, or did it survive despite institutions trying to suppress it, ignore it, or forget it? The bias profile of work that survived against institutional pressure is meaningfully different from work that survived because of institutional pressure.

**Criterion 3 — Validation across traditions.** Has the work been engaged with seriously by thinkers from outside the author's own tradition, religion, language, or political context? The more cross-validated a body of work is, the more likely it captures something that is not merely a feature of the author's local context.

**Criterion 4 — Reasoning that can be examined.** Does the work argue, or does it merely assert? Does it offer premises from which conclusions follow, or does it appeal to authority? For the purpose of training reasoning agents, only the first kind of work is useful. This excludes a great deal of revelation-based, command-based, or charisma-based moral content — not because that content is wrong, but because it cannot be reasoned from in the way the AI Parliament architecture requires.

**Criterion 5 — Specificity sufficient to constrain.** Does the work provide enough granular content to actually constrain a reasoning system, or does it provide only slogans? "Treat others as ends in themselves" is a slogan unless it comes with the Kantian apparatus that gives it teeth. The corpus must be deep enough to generate substantive disagreement when agents reason from different parts of it.

## Anticipated Objections

Four objections are foreseeable and worth addressing directly.

**Objection 1: This corpus will be Western and male.**

A reasonable concern. The seven figures above are six European men and one Andalusian Muslim. The criteria, applied without reflection, will favor traditions where individual authorship was the dominant form of moral production — which is a feature of certain literate post-axial civilizations more than others.

The response is structural, not apologetic. The criteria as written do not exclude any tradition; they only exclude *institutionally rewarded* work within any tradition. The first task of a serious curatorial body is to apply the criteria to traditions where the dominant moral mode was not individual authorship — oral traditions, indigenous knowledge systems, collective ritual practice, women's writing suppressed by all the traditions above. The criteria predict that these bodies of work, when properly excavated, will pass the filter robustly: they survived without institutional reward, often against institutional suppression.

The point is not to canonize the seven figures above. They are illustrative of the filter. The filter, applied honestly, generates a corpus far more diverse than current academic philosophy departments.

**Objection 2: Dead authors cannot consent to being used this way.**

True, and not a problem. The corpus is not a legal contract. It is a body of reasoning being drawn upon by agents who must explain their reasoning publicly. The authors are not being represented or impersonated; their arguments are being engaged with. This is what every philosophy department, every legal tradition, and every religious community has been doing with these texts for centuries. The AI Parliament does it more transparently, with better citation trails, than any human institution that has ever drawn on the same tradition.

**Objection 3: Religious traditions cannot enter the corpus on these criteria, because revelation is not a reasoning chain.**

Partially correct. The *reasoning* of theological traditions can enter — Aquinas's natural law arguments, Maimonides's legal philosophy, Al-Ghazali's epistemology of moral knowledge, Buddhist analytical traditions on consciousness and intention. The *assertions* of those traditions — the metaphysical claims that ground them — must be treated as premises whose adoption agents can argue for, not as conclusions imported wholesale. This is how legal systems already handle religious moral content. The corpus would do the same.

**Objection 4: This is just credentialing dead philosophers as the new authorities. You have not solved the problem; you have moved it.**

This is the strongest objection and deserves a direct response.

The problem has not been solved. It has been *bounded*. The selection of which obscure thinkers count is itself a curatorial decision, and that decision carries bias. The proposal does not eliminate human judgment from the loop. It changes what that judgment is selecting *for*.

Current AI safety alignment work selects, implicitly, for what credentialed contemporary thinkers in technology companies and adjacent academic institutions consider good values. That selection is performed by a narrow group operating inside intense incentive structures, with no public methodology and no accountability mechanism.

This proposal selects, openly, for what survived the filter described above, with methodology written down, with curation performed by an international body of scholars working under transparency requirements, with public logs of every inclusion and exclusion. The bias does not disappear. It becomes visible, auditable, and contestable.

That is the move. Not from biased to unbiased — from invisible bias to visible bias.

## Methodology

A serious curatorial process would have the following structure.

**An international, multi-tradition scholarly body** — independent of any government, corporation, or single religious tradition. Funded through a structure that does not allow any single donor to influence inclusion decisions. The Vatican Library, the Internet Archive, certain UNESCO heritage frameworks, and the open-source software governance models provide partial analogues. None is sufficient on its own.

**A published methodology** — the criteria above, refined and operationalized, with worked examples explaining why specific inclusions and exclusions were made. Anyone disagreeing with an inclusion can contest it in a public process. Disagreement is not blocked; it is logged.

**A versioned, immutable corpus** — every change to the corpus is recorded with reasoning, with timestamps, with the identity of curators who approved it. Earlier versions remain accessible. Agents can be run against historical versions of the corpus to verify that conclusions were not retroactively engineered.

**Mandatory cross-tradition validation** — no single tradition can have more than a defined share of the corpus. The system is structurally polycentric. The intent is not balanced representation as a political goal; it is robustness against any single tradition's blind spots becoming the system's blind spots.

**Time-locked inclusion** — works less than fifty years old are not eligible. This is a crude filter, but it serves a real function. The whole point of the corpus is that it draws on work that has survived contact with reality across generations. Recent work, however brilliant, has not yet passed that test. Recent work can inform the corpus indirectly — through commentary, through critical engagement — but it cannot enter it as a primary source. The lock is uncomfortable for living thinkers. It is also the most defensible position the methodology can take.

## The Legal Framework Required

The corpus, if built, requires legal infrastructure that does not currently exist.

It requires a charter — analogous to UNESCO conventions but with more specific governance — establishing the curatorial body, its independence requirements, its funding rules, its decision processes. It requires intellectual property frameworks that allow the corpus to be reproduced, translated, and computationally processed without violating copyright on modern translations or commentaries. It requires a designation of the corpus as a public commons, protected from acquisition by any single state or company. It requires sanctions for tampering — civil and, in extreme cases, criminal liability for unauthorized modification of corpus content used in deployed governance systems.

None of this exists. The EU AI Act, the most ambitious AI legislation in the world, has nothing to say about it. Neither does any national AI strategy. The legal architecture for governance-grade moral knowledge bases is a vacuum.

That vacuum is the work.

## What This Platform Will Do

WLS will, over the coming years, contribute to this work in several specific ways.

It will publish detailed analyses of candidate works for the corpus — arguing for and against specific inclusions, applying the criteria above to concrete texts, building up a public record of the kind of reasoning the methodology requires.

It will engage with the international scholarly community on the methodology itself — refining the criteria, identifying edge cases, building toward a draft charter that could be presented to UNESCO, the Council of Europe, or other multilateral bodies as a starting point.

It will document the institutional resistance the project will face. There will be resistance. The methodology directly threatens the position of every existing power center that benefits from being the implicit source of AI alignment values. The resistance itself is data. It needs to be on the record.

And it will do this slowly. The work is generational. The corpus, properly built, is not finished in a decade. The institutions that need to be created are not finished in a generation. The platform's posture is that this is acceptable — that the alternative, which is to let the moral foundations of AI governance be set by whoever moves first under current incentive structures, is not acceptable.

The seven thinkers above wrote for audiences that did not exist in their lifetimes. They wrote because the work was worth doing whether anyone was listening or not.

That posture is the moral foundation of this project. It is also the criterion the project must meet itself.
