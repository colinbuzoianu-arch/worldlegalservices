# AI as a Peace Tool: The One Application Nobody Is Building

*worldlegalservices.com — Global Peace Frameworks Series, Article 6*

---

Artificial intelligence is being built into weapons systems, surveillance infrastructure, autonomous lethal platforms, disinformation generation tools, and cyber attack capabilities at a pace that exceeds the development of any legal or governance framework designed to constrain these applications.

This is the dominant narrative of AI and international security — and it is accurate as far as it goes. The military and adversarial applications of AI are real, consequential, and dangerously under-governed.

But there is another side of the relationship between AI and international peace that receives almost no serious institutional attention: the potential of AI systems to strengthen the international frameworks that prevent conflict, support the monitoring and verification of arms control agreements, provide early warning of atrocity risk, and enable the kind of structured, evidence-based international deliberation that the current architecture of international institutions is structurally incapable of providing.

These applications are technically feasible. They are institutionally neglected. The asymmetry between the resources devoted to AI for conflict and the resources devoted to AI for peace is one of the most consequential misallocations in contemporary policy.

---

## Early Warning: Where AI Is Already Demonstrating Value

The failure to prevent mass atrocities is one of the most persistent and most documented failures of the international system. Rwanda, Srebrenica, Darfur, Rohingya — each preceded by warning signs that were visible in advance and inadequately acted upon. The failure was not primarily informational — the information existed. It was institutional and political: the mechanisms for translating warning into protective action were absent or paralysed.

AI-assisted early warning systems represent the most mature application of AI to peace and security — the area where proof of concept already exists and where the gap between what is technically possible and what is institutionally deployed is most directly addressable.

The Atrocity Forecasting Project and similar academic initiatives have demonstrated that machine learning models trained on historical conflict data can produce statistically significant predictions of mass atrocity risk — not as precise forecasts but as probabilistic risk assessments that outperform the baseline of expert intuition alone. The United Nations Office on Genocide Prevention has developed frameworks for risk assessment that could be augmented by AI-assisted analysis of the data streams — social media, satellite imagery, economic indicators, political event data — that are already being monitored by academic and NGO researchers.

The gap is not technical. It is institutional. Converting AI-assisted risk assessment into protective action requires political will, Security Council cooperation, and rapid deployment capacity — exactly the institutional capabilities that the UN system struggles most to provide. AI can improve the quality of warning. It cannot, by itself, solve the political problems that have historically prevented warning from becoming protection.

What it can do is change the political calculation by making inaction harder to justify. When the evidence of atrocity risk is systematically compiled, continuously updated, and publicly accessible — when decision-makers cannot credibly claim they did not see it coming — the political cost of inaction increases. That change in political cost is real and it is achievable with available technology.

## Arms Control Verification: The Technical Opportunity

The history of arms control is in part a history of verification failures — agreements that were made and then violated because the monitoring capacity to detect violations was insufficient, or because the political will to act on detected violations was absent.

AI offers genuinely transformative potential for arms control verification — the technical capacity to monitor compliance with arms control agreements at a level of comprehensiveness and continuity that was previously impossible.

Satellite imagery analysis — using AI to process the continuous stream of commercial and intelligence satellite data — can provide near-real-time monitoring of military installations, weapons production facilities, and deployment patterns at a scale and cost that human analysts working with traditional methods cannot match. The technical capacity already exists in commercial form; its application to arms control verification is a policy choice, not a technical limitation.

Open source intelligence — the systematic analysis of publicly available data including social media, procurement records, shipping manifests, and technical publications — can provide indicators of weapons development activity that supplements satellite imagery and signals intelligence. AI-assisted OSINT analysis has already demonstrated its value in tracking the movements of military forces and weapons systems in the Ukraine conflict.

Nuclear verification is perhaps the highest-value application. The NPT's verification regime, administered by the IAEA, depends on periodic inspections and reporting requirements that states in violation — as North Korea, Iraq, Libya, and Iran have demonstrated in different ways — can evade or manipulate. Continuous AI-assisted monitoring of indicators associated with nuclear weapons development would not make evasion impossible, but it would make it significantly harder and more expensive. The political commitment to developing and deploying such systems has not matched the technical opportunity.

## Conflict Prevention: Structured Deliberation at Scale

The most ambitious application of AI to international peace — and the one most directly connected to the WLS governance framework — is the use of AI-assisted deliberation to support the kind of structured, evidence-based international dialogue that current institutions fail to provide.

International diplomacy is constrained by the same institutional failures that affect domestic governance: it is conducted by human actors with career incentives, national interests, and domestic political pressures that systematically diverge from the optimal outcomes for international stability. Diplomatic negotiations are slow, opaque, and shaped by the asymmetry of resources between powerful and weak states.

An AI-assisted deliberation system — operating on the principles of the AI Parliament framework described in this series — could provide several capabilities that current diplomatic architecture lacks.

**Scenario modelling.** In a crisis situation, AI systems could rapidly model the consequences of different response options — including the escalation dynamics, the humanitarian impacts, the economic consequences, and the legal implications of each option — and make this modelling available to all parties and to the public. The asymmetry of analytical capacity between powerful and weak states in international negotiations is itself a driver of unjust outcomes; AI-assisted analysis available to all parties would partially address this asymmetry.

**Historical pattern analysis.** AI systems trained on the history of international crises and their resolutions could identify analogies, precedents, and failure modes relevant to current situations — providing a systematic institutional memory that is currently unavailable in a form that decision-makers can access in real time.

**Compliance monitoring.** For agreements that have been reached, AI-assisted monitoring could provide the continuous verification capacity that makes agreement more credible and violation more costly — addressing the commitment problem that makes states reluctant to make agreements they fear others will not honour.

**Transparent documentation.** The opacity of international negotiations — conducted behind closed doors, with positions obscured by diplomatic language — is itself a driver of public distrust and political difficulty in implementing agreements. AI-assisted documentation and translation of negotiating positions, made available in accessible form to affected publics, would increase the democratic accountability of international diplomacy without compromising the confidentiality that genuine negotiation requires.

## Why This Is Not Being Built

The applications described above are technically feasible. They are not being built, or are being built only at the margins of institutional attention, for reasons that deserve examination.

**The peace application of AI has no constituency with resources.** The military applications of AI are funded by defence budgets of hundreds of billions of dollars annually. The peace applications are funded by foundations, academic grants, and the discretionary budgets of underfunded international institutions. The resource asymmetry is not incidental — it reflects the political economy of who benefits from each category of application.

**The states with the most capacity to build these systems have mixed incentives.** A state with superior AI capabilities for hybrid warfare, autonomous weapons, and intelligence gathering has limited interest in building AI systems that would improve the monitoring of its own compliance with arms control agreements or increase the transparency of its diplomatic positions. The states best positioned to build AI peace tools are also the states whose strategic advantage depends on information asymmetry that those tools would reduce.

**International institutions lack the technical capacity and the mandate.** The UN system and its specialised agencies operate with budgets and staffing that are inadequate for the technical development required. The mandate questions — who authorises the deployment of AI systems in international security contexts, who controls the data they generate, who governs their outputs — have not been resolved and create institutional paralysis.

**The governance framework for AI peace tools does not exist.** Ironically, the absence of a legal framework for AI in governance — documented in the AI Governance series on this platform — is itself a barrier to the development of AI peace tools. Without clarity on the legal status of AI-assisted analysis in international decision-making, the accountability frameworks for AI-generated recommendations, and the institutional governance of AI systems operating in international security contexts, even willing institutions face significant obstacles to deployment.

## A Call for Serious Attention

The application of AI to peace and security has been dominated by the weapons applications — because weapons attract funding, because weapons development serves the short-term strategic interests of powerful states, and because the institutional architecture for developing AI peace tools does not yet exist.

Building that institutional architecture is a policy choice. It requires the same combination of political will, civil society pressure, and legal institution-building that has produced every other advance in international humanitarian law — none of which happened because powerful states chose to constrain themselves voluntarily, all of which happened because sustained pressure from states, institutions, and civil society made the cost of inaction higher than the cost of commitment.

The technology exists. The need is urgent. The gap between them is institutional and political.

Closing that gap is work that this platform was built to contribute to.
