# Climate Displacement and the Refugee Law Vacuum

*worldlegalservice.com — Global Peace Frameworks Series, Article 5*

---

The 1951 Refugee Convention defines a refugee as a person who has fled their country owing to a well-founded fear of persecution on grounds of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion.

The definition was written for a specific historical moment: the displacement of millions of Europeans in the aftermath of the Second World War, by state actors engaged in deliberate persecution. It has served, imperfectly and unevenly, as the legal foundation of international refugee protection for over seventy years.

It has nothing to say about a Bangladeshi farmer whose land has been submerged by sea level rise. It has nothing to say about a Somali family whose livelihood has been destroyed by a decade of drought driven by global temperature increases. It has nothing to say about a Pacific Islander whose island nation will cease to exist within decades. These people are displaced. They are not persecuted in any sense the 1951 Convention recognises. They have no legal status under international refugee law.

They are, by the most conservative estimates, a preview of a population that will number in the hundreds of millions by the end of this century.

International law has no framework for them. The conversation about building one has barely begun.

---

## The Scale of What Is Coming

The relationship between climate change and human displacement is not speculative. It is already observable, in slow-moving ways that will accelerate dramatically as temperature increases compound.

Sea level rise threatens coastal and low-lying areas where hundreds of millions of people live — from Bangladesh to Vietnam to the Pacific island states to the coastlines of Africa, South America, and Southeast Asia. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's projections indicate that under current emissions trajectories, several hundred million people face displacement from coastal flooding by 2100.

Desertification and water stress are already driving displacement across the Sahel, the Horn of Africa, and parts of the Middle East and Central Asia. The connection between climate stress, agricultural failure, livelihood collapse, and onward migration — through conflict, through economic desperation, through the simple impossibility of remaining where survival is no longer possible — is well-documented and intensifying.

Extreme weather events — hurricanes, floods, wildfires — are increasing in frequency and intensity. The displacement they cause is often temporary but increasingly permanent as affected areas become uninhabitable or uninsurable at the level required for reconstruction.

The World Bank's Groundswell report estimated in 2021 that without significant climate action and development support, up to 216 million people could be internally displaced within their own countries by 2050 due to slow-onset climate impacts alone. This figure does not include cross-border displacement.

None of these people have a legal status under international refugee law. None have a legal right to protection in any state other than their own. None can invoke the non-refoulement principle — the cornerstone of refugee law, prohibiting return to a place where a person faces serious harm — because their harm is not persecution in the Convention sense.

---

## Why the Gap Has Not Been Filled

The legal vacuum around climate displacement is not an oversight. It is the product of deliberate choices made by states that understand the scale of what is coming and have calculated that acknowledging legal obligations toward climate-displaced people would create commitments they are unwilling to accept.

The dynamics are similar to those that have blocked other international legal frameworks in this series: the states with the most power to create binding instruments are also the states with the most to lose from accepting legal obligations toward the populations most affected by climate change — which are overwhelmingly in the Global South, while the historical emissions most responsible for climate change are overwhelmingly from the Global North.

The UNFCCC negotiations have developed the concept of Loss and Damage — the acknowledgment that climate change causes losses and damages to vulnerable countries that adaptation cannot prevent — and the COP27 in 2022 produced a breakthrough agreement to establish a Loss and Damage fund. The fund's capitalisation has been slow and its legal architecture does not include any individual protection rights for displaced people.

The New York Declaration for Refugees and Migrants of 2016 acknowledged climate change as a driver of displacement and committed to developing pathways for climate-related displacement. It produced the Global Compact on Refugees in 2018 — a non-binding framework that mentions environmental degradation as a displacement driver but does not create legal status or protection rights for climate-displaced people.

The Nansen Initiative and its successor the Platform on Disaster Displacement have developed guidance on protection for people displaced across borders in the context of disasters and climate change. The guidance is non-binding and not widely implemented.

The cumulative result: a series of acknowledgments, commitments, and non-binding frameworks that recognise the problem without creating the legal obligations that would address it.

## The Small Island State Case

The most acute and legally clear-cut dimension of the climate displacement problem is the situation of small island developing states facing the complete submersion of their territory.

Kiribati, Tuvalu, the Marshall Islands, and several other Pacific island nations face the prospect of territorial inundation within decades under current sea level rise projections. For these states, climate displacement is not a risk to be managed — it is an existential certainty to be prepared for.

The legal questions raised by the disappearance of a state's territory are profound and unanswered. Can a state without territory continue to exist as a legal entity? Can it maintain its UN membership, its exclusive economic zone, its treaty rights? What happens to the legal status of its nationals when the state itself has no land?

The ICJ is currently developing an advisory opinion on the obligations of states with respect to climate change — requested by the UN General Assembly following advocacy by Pacific island states. The opinion will not resolve the displacement question directly, but its reasoning may clarify the legal obligations of major emitting states toward the populations most affected by their emissions.

The political declaration signed by Tuvalu and Australia in 2023 — offering a pathway for Tuvaluan citizens to live, work, and study in Australia — represents a bilateral response to the territorial extinction problem. It is a precedent of a kind, but it does not constitute a legal framework and it depends on the political willingness of receiving states that has no guarantee of continuity.

---

## What a Framework Would Look Like

A legal framework for climate displacement does not need to resolve every question before it can begin to provide protection. It needs to establish, at minimum, three things.

**Legal status.** People displaced across borders by climate impacts need a legal status that entitles them to protection — not necessarily the full refugee status of the 1951 Convention, but a defined status that triggers specific obligations on the part of receiving states: non-refoulement, access to documentation, the right to work, access to basic services. The absence of any status is what leaves these people in a legal void.

**Responsibility allocation.** The question of which states bear responsibility for hosting climate-displaced populations — and the related question of financial responsibility between the states historically most responsible for emissions and the states geographically most affected by displacement — needs a legal framework. The current ad hoc arrangement, in which displacement falls on whatever state a displaced person reaches, produces both humanitarian failures and the political backlash that produces anti-immigration politics across receiving countries.

**Planned relocation mechanisms.** For the small island states and low-lying coastal populations where displacement is not a risk but a certainty, planned relocation — with legal protections, cultural continuity provisions, and the maintenance of collective identity — needs international legal support. The bilateral arrangements being negotiated by Pacific island states represent a starting point; they need to be multilateralised and made legally durable.

---

## The Peace Connection

Climate displacement is included in a global peace frameworks series because its consequences for international peace and security are direct and documented.

The relationship between climate stress, livelihood collapse, state fragility, and armed conflict has been studied extensively. The Syrian civil war, which generated the largest refugee crisis in Europe since the Second World War, was preceded by the worst drought in the region's recorded history. The conflicts in the Sahel — Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger — have intensified as desertification and competition over diminishing agricultural resources have driven displacement and grievance.

Climate displacement does not cause conflict mechanically or inevitably. It creates the conditions — resource stress, mass movement of people, state incapacity, grievance — in which conflict becomes more likely. Managing those conditions requires both the mitigation of climate change itself and the legal frameworks that provide orderly, protected pathways for the displacement that is already unavoidable.

The alternative — allowing climate displacement to accelerate without legal frameworks, forcing hundreds of millions of people into the informal, unprotected movement of people with no legal status and no receiving state obligations — is a recipe for the kind of humanitarian and political crises that have already strained the foundations of the international order.

Building the legal framework now, before the full scale of displacement materialises, is immeasurably preferable to building it in response to crises that will be far harder to manage than the framework that prevented them would have been to construct.
