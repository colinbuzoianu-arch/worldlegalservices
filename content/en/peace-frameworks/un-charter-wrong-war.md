# The UN Charter Was Written for a War That Already Happened

*worldlegalservice.com — Global Peace Frameworks Series, Article 1*

---

The United Nations Charter was signed in San Francisco on June 26, 1945. The war in Europe had ended six weeks earlier. The war in the Pacific would end six weeks later. The document was written by people who had just lived through the worst violence in human history and were determined, with genuine conviction, to build an architecture that would prevent it from happening again.

They built it for the war they had just survived. They could not build it for the wars that would follow — because those wars had not yet happened, and the strategic imagination of 1945, however capacious, could not fully anticipate the forms that organised violence would take in the decades ahead.

Eighty years later, the Charter remains the foundational document of international peace and security law. It has not been substantively amended since 1965, when Article 23 was modified to expand the Security Council from eleven to fifteen members. The world it was designed to govern no longer exists. The architecture it created governs — or fails to govern — a world it was never designed for.

---

## What the Charter Was Built to Prevent

The Charter's architects had a specific failure in mind: the failure of the League of Nations to prevent the Second World War. The League had collapsed for identifiable reasons — the absence of major powers, the unanimity requirement that made collective action impossible, the lack of enforcement capacity, the weakness of the collective security commitment.

The Charter addressed each of these failures directly. The UN would include all major powers, including the wartime allies. The Security Council would have primary responsibility for international peace and security, with five permanent members — the United States, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, France, and China — holding veto power. The veto was not a design flaw; it was the price of major power participation. Without the veto, the great powers would not have joined. With the veto, they joined an institution they could never be outvoted in on matters of vital national interest.

The Charter prohibited the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state — Article 2(4), the most important provision in the document. It provided two exceptions: self-defense under Article 51, and collective security action authorised by the Security Council under Chapter VII.

This architecture was designed for a specific threat model: a state using conventional military force to invade another state, triggering a collective response coordinated through the Security Council.

---

## Why the Architecture Fails

The threat model the Charter was designed for has not disappeared. But it has been joined by a range of other threats for which the Charter provides no adequate framework — and the one threat the Charter was designed to prevent has itself evolved in ways the veto system cannot handle.

**The veto deadlock.** The permanent five were chosen because they were the victorious great powers of 1945. They remain permanent members regardless of how the global balance of power has shifted in eighty years. More consequentially: any permanent member can veto Security Council action against itself or its allies, regardless of the evidence of a violation. Russia invaded Ukraine — a clear violation of Article 2(4) — and vetoed every Security Council resolution that would have authorized a collective response. The Charter's primary enforcement mechanism was paralysed at the moment it was most needed by the very veto provision that was designed to make major power participation possible.

This is not a malfunction. It is the system working as designed — which is the problem. The design assumption was that the great powers would maintain sufficient common interest in international order to use the Security Council cooperatively. That assumption held imperfectly during the Cold War and has broken down more completely since.

**Hybrid warfare.** The Charter was written for armies crossing borders. It has almost nothing to say about cyberattacks on critical infrastructure, disinformation campaigns designed to destabilise democratic institutions, economic coercion used as a geopolitical weapon, proxy warfare conducted through non-state actors, or the weaponisation of migration flows. These tools of conflict have been used extensively and systematically in the past two decades by state actors who have calculated, correctly, that they fall outside the Charter's prohibition on the use of force or at least in a legal grey zone where attribution and response are both contested.

The international law of hybrid warfare is in its infancy. The Charter provides no framework. Custom is developing slowly, through state practice and academic debate, far behind the operational reality of the conflicts it is supposed to govern.

**Non-state actors.** Al-Qaeda, ISIS, the Wagner Group, Hezbollah, Hamas — the most significant organised violence of the past three decades has been perpetrated by actors who are not states and who therefore fall outside the Charter's state-centric framework. The Charter's prohibition on the use of force applies to states. Its collective security mechanisms are designed to respond to state aggression. The legal basis for military action against non-state actors operating from the territory of a state that is either unwilling or unable to suppress them — the doctrine invoked for the wars in Afghanistan and Syria and many others — is contested, inconsistently applied, and nowhere clearly defined in the Charter.

**Great power competition below the threshold.** The most consequential geopolitical competition of the current era — between the United States and China over Taiwan, trade, technology, and regional influence — is conducted through means that are simultaneously profoundly destabilising and largely outside the Charter's prohibitions. Economic coercion, technological competition, military posturing, alliance building, influence operations — none of these clearly violate Article 2(4), and none are subject to Security Council oversight in any meaningful sense.

---

## The Reform Paralysis

The Charter can be amended — Article 108 specifies the procedure. It requires a two-thirds majority of the General Assembly, including ratification by all five permanent members.

In practice, the amendment procedure has been used once in eighty years. Any amendment that would constrain the veto power of the permanent five requires their agreement. No permanent member has ever agreed to, or will agree to, an amendment that reduces its own power. The reform paralysis is therefore structural and permanent under the current framework.

Proposals for reform have been made continuously and seriously: expanding the Security Council to include contemporary great powers like India, Brazil, Japan, and Germany; creating a two-tier veto structure that would allow collective action in cases of mass atrocities even over permanent member objection; establishing an independent body to assess the legality of uses of force. Each proposal has attracted significant support. None has been implemented.

The gap between what the UN system does and what international peace and security requires grows each year that reform does not happen.

---

## What Survives

It would be a mistake to conclude from this analysis that the UN Charter has failed entirely or that the institutions it created have no value.

The Charter established norms — the prohibition on aggressive war, the principle of sovereign equality, the framework of human rights — that have shaped state behaviour in real ways even when enforcement has been absent. States that violate these norms bear reputational and political costs, even when they bear no legal consequences. The existence of the norm matters, even when its enforcement is imperfect.

The UN's specialised agencies — WHO, UNHCR, WFP, UNICEF — have delivered real and substantial humanitarian benefit, operating largely outside the political constraints that paralyse the Security Council.

The International Court of Justice has developed a body of international law — on territorial disputes, on treaty interpretation, on state responsibility — that has genuine authority even without a reliable enforcement mechanism.

These are not nothing. They are the surviving institutional inheritance of 1945, still functioning in the areas where the Charter architecture works well enough.

---

## The Design Question for the Next Architecture

The honest assessment of the UN Charter is that it represents a serious and partially successful attempt to institutionalise the lessons of one specific historical experience of catastrophic international failure. It has aged better than it might have. It has aged worse than its designers hoped.

The question for the current generation is not how to reform an institution whose reform mechanism is structurally blocked. It is what principles should govern the next attempt at international peace architecture — whenever the combination of great power conflict, technological change, and institutional failure creates the political conditions for building something new.

The WLS framework's answer draws on the same principles that govern the AI governance analysis: accountability must be real rather than formal; enforcement must not depend on the voluntary compliance of those with the most power to violate the rules; and the architecture must be designed for the threats that exist rather than the threats of a previous era.
