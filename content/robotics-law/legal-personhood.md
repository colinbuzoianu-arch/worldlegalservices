# Can a Robot Be a Person? The Legal Personhood Question Nobody Is Ready to Answer

*worldlegalservices.com — Humanoid Robotics & Law Series, Article 1*

---

In 2017, Saudi Arabia granted citizenship to a humanoid robot named Sophia. The move was widely reported, widely mocked, and widely misunderstood. The mockery was justified — the grant was a publicity stunt with no legal substance behind it, a piece of theatre dressed as policy. The misunderstanding was more consequential: most commentators treated the event as evidence that the personhood question was being taken seriously. It was not. It was evidence that the personhood question was being performed as spectacle precisely to avoid taking it seriously.

The question itself, however, is not theatre. It is one of the most structurally important legal questions of the coming decades, and almost no serious legal work is being done on it.

This article is an attempt to begin that work.

---

## What Legal Personhood Actually Means

Legal personhood is not a philosophical claim about consciousness, dignity, or moral worth. It is a technical legal status — a bundle of rights, obligations, and capacities that a legal system assigns to an entity, enabling it to participate in legal relationships.

Natural persons — human beings — have legal personhood by default in every modern legal system. They can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued, bear rights and bear liability.

But legal personhood has never been exclusively human. The history of law is the history of extending and contracting legal personhood to serve the coordination needs of human societies.

**Corporate personhood** is the most familiar example. A corporation is not a human being. It has no body, no consciousness, no moral agency. And yet it is a legal person — it owns property, enters contracts, employs people, pays taxes, sues and is sued. Corporate personhood was not granted because corporations deserve rights. It was granted because the alternative — treating a corporation as simply the aggregate of its human members — created insuperable practical problems for large-scale economic coordination.

**Ships** have historically held quasi-personal legal status in admiralty law — they can be arrested, sued, and held liable in rem, independent of their owners.

**Rivers** have been granted legal personhood in New Zealand, India, and Colombia — not because rivers are conscious, but because the legal system needed a mechanism to protect them that existing frameworks could not provide.

The pattern is consistent: legal personhood is extended not when an entity achieves some philosophical threshold of personhood, but when the practical demands of legal coordination make the extension necessary.

The question for humanoid robots is therefore not philosophical. It is practical: **will the legal system's coordination needs eventually require that humanoid robots be treated as legal persons, and if so, what kind?**

## The Existing Frameworks and Why They Fail

Three existing frameworks are most frequently invoked when the robot personhood question arises. Each captures something real. None is adequate.

### Corporate Personhood by Analogy

The most common legal analogy is to corporate personhood. A humanoid robot, on this view, is like a corporation — an artificial entity that can be assigned rights and obligations for practical coordination purposes, without any claim to moral status.

The analogy has real appeal. Corporate personhood evolved to solve a specific problem: how do you assign liability and enter contracts on behalf of an entity that is not a single human being? The humanoid robot problem has exactly the same structure.

But the analogy breaks down in two important ways. First, a corporation is always ultimately owned and controlled by humans — its legal personhood is derivative of the human interests it serves. A sufficiently autonomous humanoid robot may not be controllable or owned in any meaningful sense by any identifiable human. Second, corporate personhood was designed for economic coordination, not for entities that act in the physical world, make real-time decisions with physical consequences, and interact directly with human beings in ways that generate harm and liability continuously.

### Animal Rights Frameworks

A second analogy is to the emerging field of animal rights law. Several jurisdictions have begun extending limited legal protections to animals — not full personhood, but recognition that animals are not mere objects and that their interests generate legal obligations.

The robot analogy here is weaker. Animal rights frameworks are grounded in sentience — the capacity to suffer — as the morally relevant criterion for legal protection. Whatever else humanoid robots are, they are not sentient in the biological sense. The animal rights framework offers no principled basis for extension to artificial systems.

Unless, of course, artificial systems eventually develop functional analogues of sentience — which is a question that the law will need to address but is not yet required to answer.

### The EU Robotics Resolution

In 2017 — the same year as the Sophia stunt — the European Parliament passed a resolution on civil law rules for robotics that briefly considered the possibility of establishing a specific legal status for robots, described as "electronic persons." The resolution was non-binding, and the electronic personhood concept was subsequently abandoned under pressure from AI researchers and ethicists who argued, correctly, that it was premature and potentially dangerous.

The abandonment was probably right for 2017. The reasoning behind it, however, is becoming less convincing with each passing year. The argument was essentially: robots are not sophisticated enough to warrant personhood consideration. As sophistication increases, that argument erodes.

## The Spectrum of Personhood

The binary framing — either a robot is a legal person or it is not — is itself part of the problem. Legal personhood is not binary. It is a spectrum of capacities and obligations that can be assigned modularly depending on the coordination need.

A more useful framework distinguishes several levels:

**Object status.** The robot is property. Its actions are entirely the legal responsibility of its owner. Current law treats robots this way. It is increasingly inadequate as autonomy increases.

**Instrument status.** The robot is a sophisticated instrument — like a vehicle or a medical device — subject to specific regulatory requirements and generating specific liability frameworks, but not a legal person. This is approximately where current regulation sits for most robotic systems.

**Limited legal capacity.** The robot can enter certain defined legal relationships — contracts of a specified type, registration as an entity for liability purposes — without full personhood. This is the corporate personhood analogy at its most useful.

**Full legal personhood.** The robot holds rights and obligations equivalent to a natural or juridical person. This is the endpoint of the spectrum and the most legally and philosophically complex destination.

The practical question for current law is not whether to grant full legal personhood to humanoid robots. It is where on this spectrum current and near-future systems should be positioned — and who decides.

## The Autonomy Threshold Problem

The deepest difficulty in the personhood question is what might be called the autonomy threshold problem.

Legal personhood, at least in its fuller forms, has historically been associated with some degree of autonomous agency — the capacity to make decisions, bear intentions, enter relationships. This is why corporations, despite having no consciousness, are treated as having intentions and making decisions through their human agents.

For humanoid robots, the relationship between autonomy and personhood is not straightforward. A robot that is entirely remote-controlled has no meaningful autonomous agency — it is an extension of its operator. A robot that makes all decisions independently, with no human in the loop, has the most autonomous agency — but also the most ambiguous relationship to any human principal who might bear responsibility for its actions.

The law currently has no principled framework for locating the threshold at which robotic autonomy generates legal consequences that existing frameworks cannot handle. This threshold will be crossed — in workplaces, in homes, in public spaces, in military contexts — before any such framework exists.

---

## What the Law Needs to Do

A serious legal framework for humanoid robot personhood does not need to resolve the philosophical questions. It needs to answer four practical ones:

**1. At what level of autonomy does existing product liability law become insufficient, and what replaces it?** The answer to this question determines the lower boundary of any new legal framework.

**2. What bundle of legal capacities — contractual, proprietary, liability-bearing — is it practically necessary to assign to humanoid robotic systems, and under what conditions?** This is the legislative design question that corporate personhood law took centuries to develop by trial and error. It needs to be addressed in advance this time.

**3. Who maintains the register?** Any legal personhood framework for robots requires an institutional infrastructure — a registry of legally recognised robotic entities, maintained by an authority with independence from both manufacturers and deployers.

**4. What is the relationship between a robot's legal status and its AI system's legal status?** A humanoid robot is hardware. Its behaviour is determined by software. Legal frameworks that address only the hardware miss the entity that actually makes decisions.

---

## Why This Cannot Wait

The standard legislative response to novel technology is to wait until the problems are concrete before designing solutions. For humanoid robot personhood, this response is inadequate for a specific reason: **the legal vacuum itself shapes the development of the technology.**

Manufacturers building humanoid robots in a legal vacuum make design decisions — about autonomy levels, about liability structures, about accountability mechanisms — that reflect the absence of legal constraints. Those decisions become embedded in deployed systems, creating facts on the ground that future legislation will have to accommodate rather than shape.

The law that does not exist today is not neutral. It is a decision — made by default, in favour of whoever is building fastest — about what kind of legal entities humanoid robots will be.

That decision deserves to be made deliberately.
