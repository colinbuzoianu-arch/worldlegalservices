# From the Periphery to the Center: Why Eastern Europe Must Lead the Next Governance Revolution

*worldlegalservices.com — Eastern European Angle Series, Article 6*

---

There is a map of the world that international relations scholars call the "core-periphery" model. The core — wealthy, technologically advanced, institutionally stable — sets the terms of global governance. The periphery — poorer, less institutionally developed, less technologically capable — adopts, adapts, and sometimes resists those terms, but rarely shapes them. Eastern Europe, in this model, sits somewhere between the two: part of the core's institutional alliance through EU and NATO membership, but not quite the core — not quite the source of the frameworks and ideas that govern the global system.

This series has argued, article by article, that this positioning is wrong — not merely geopolitically but epistemically. Eastern Europe is not a late adopter of governance models developed elsewhere. It is a laboratory that has generated governance knowledge of the highest importance — knowledge about institutional failure, about the limits of formal democracy, about what makes accountability mechanisms resilient, about how civic society functions as the last check on captured institutions. That knowledge was generated at extraordinary cost. It has not been adequately applied.

This final article argues that the next governance revolution — the transition from 20th century political institutions to governance architectures adequate for the 21st — will require Eastern European knowledge at its center, not its periphery. And it maps what taking that position seriously would look like.

---

## The Knowledge Advantage

Eastern Europe's governance knowledge advantage is specific, not general. It is not that Eastern Europeans are wiser or more virtuous than people elsewhere — that claim would be false and patronising. It is that Eastern Europe has lived through specific institutional experiences that have generated specific insights unavailable to societies that have not had those experiences.

**The experience of institutional lying at scale.** Societies that have lived under systems where state institutions systematically lied — about economic performance, about political opposition, about the consequences of policy decisions — develop a cultural immunity to official narrative that is structurally different from the healthy scepticism of a free press tradition. It is more visceral, more historically grounded, and more alert to the specific mechanisms through which institutional communication becomes institutional deception.

This is directly relevant to the AI governance challenge. The risk of AI systems deployed in governance roles is not primarily that they will be technically unreliable. It is that they will become instruments of a new form of institutional lying — systems that produce the appearance of objective, accountable governance while being designed or operated to serve the interests of whoever controls them. Eastern Europeans have a trained sensitivity to this risk that is not learned from books.

**The experience of formal institutions that do not function as described.** Every post-communist society has experience of constitutions that guaranteed rights that were not protected, courts that were described as independent but were not, elections that were described as free but were not. This experience produces a capacity to distinguish between institutional form and institutional substance — to ask not what an institution claims to do but what it actually does, who benefits from the gap between the claim and the reality, and what structural changes would close it.

**The experience of building from scratch.** Estonia built digital governance on a blank slate. Romania rebuilt judicial institutions after communism. Poland created an independent civil service from nothing. The experience of institutional construction — not reform of existing systems, but design and building of new ones — is directly applicable to the AI governance challenge, which requires not reforming the institutions of the 20th century but building the institutions of the 21st.

---

## The Specific Contributions

Eastern Europe's contribution to the next governance revolution is not merely experiential. It is specific and practical.

**Digital governance architecture.** Estonia's X-Road, e-ID, and data transparency model represents the world's most advanced practical implementation of the principles that AI governance requires: distributed architecture, citizen-accessible audit trails, resilience by design. Scaling and adapting this model for AI governance contexts is work that Estonian expertise is uniquely positioned to lead.

**Anti-corruption architecture under adversarial conditions.** Romania's DNA — the National Anticorruption Directorate — despite its subsequent political controversies, represents a serious attempt to build prosecutorial independence against intense political pressure. Its successes and failures are a direct source of design lessons for the independence mechanisms that AI governance systems require. Poland's experience rebuilding judicial independence after PiS-era damage is a real-time case study in institutional repair that will generate lessons applicable far beyond Poland.

**Civil society as governance actor.** Eastern European civil society — forged under communist suppression, tested by post-communist governance failures, demonstrated in mass mobilisations from Timișoara to Kyiv — has a practical understanding of how civic organisation functions as a governance accountability mechanism that civil society in more institutionally stable environments has not needed to develop to the same depth.

**The Visegrád critical voice.** Hungary's and Poland's challenges to EU institutional overreach — however mixed their motivations — have generated serious arguments about subsidiarity, democratic legitimacy, and the limits of supranational governance that the EU needs to engage with honestly rather than dismiss as obstruction. The critique of Brussels from Warsaw and Budapest is sometimes bad faith and sometimes genuine democratic concern. Separating the two requires taking the arguments seriously — which produces a more rigorous analysis of EU institutional design than the comfortable consensus of Western European capitals tends to generate.

## What Leadership Actually Looks Like

Eastern Europe leading the next governance revolution does not mean Eastern European governments dominating international institutions — they do not have the geopolitical weight for that, and weight-based institutional dominance is precisely the model the WLS framework is designed to replace.

It means Eastern European thinkers, analysts, technologists, and civil society actors contributing their specific knowledge to the international governance conversation from a position of confidence rather than deference.

It means research institutions in Warsaw, Tallinn, Bucharest, Bratislava, and Kyiv developing AI governance research programs that draw explicitly on the region's institutional experience — not as historical curiosity but as live analytical resource applied to current governance design challenges.

It means Eastern European governments proposing, within EU and international processes, specific governance design requirements informed by the region's experience of institutional failure — requirements for structural independence, for genuine auditability, for accountability mechanisms that operate at the speed of institutional capture rather than the speed of diplomatic process.

It means the community of Eastern European diaspora professionals in technology, law, finance, and policy — spread across Western Europe, North America, and Asia — bringing their regional institutional knowledge into the global governance conversations they participate in.

And it means platforms like this one — built in Timișoara, writing from the specific institutional experience of a country that has lived through communism, revolution, imperfect transition, and ongoing governance struggle — contributing to the global analysis of governance challenges with the confidence that the experience this region carries is not a liability to be apologised for but an asset to be deployed.

## The WLS Project as Eastern European Governance Initiative

World Legal Services is, among other things, an Eastern European governance initiative. It was conceived in Romania. Its founding voice draws on the specific epistemic resources of a post-communist society — the trained scepticism about institutional claims, the visceral understanding of what governance failure costs, the capacity to distinguish institutional form from institutional substance that living inside a system that lied produces.

The AI Parliament concept — governance by structured deliberation among agents bound to an immutable moral knowledge base, with no single agent able to dominate, with all reasoning auditable — is not a technology project with governance implications. It is a governance design project that responds to specific known failure modes with specific architectural solutions. The failure modes it responds to are the ones Eastern Europe has documented most thoroughly: institutional capture, narrative control, the gap between accountability claim and accountability reality.

The economic layer — knowledge-backed tokens, non-transferable and time-limited, earned through demonstrated understanding of foundational principles — responds to the specific failure mode of wealth as governance access. In every Eastern European post-communist transition, the most consequential governance failures were produced by the intersection of economic power and political influence. The WLS economic architecture is designed structurally to prevent that intersection.

The learning platform — the curriculum that teaches the foundational principles of AI governance, legal frameworks, and the history of governance failure — is, in its Eastern European dimension, the institutionalisation of the knowledge that this region has generated at such cost: made accessible, structured, and transmissible to the global community of people who want to participate in building something better.

## The Invitation

This series has covered thirty years of Eastern European governance experience — the successes of the Baltic digital states, the institutional lessons of the Romanian transition, the democratic backsliding of Hungary and Poland, the civic revolutions from Timișoara to the Maidan, the accession lever's successes and limits.

The invitation this series extends is simple: take this knowledge seriously. Not as regional history. As governance science — tested under the hardest possible conditions, applicable to the most important challenges of the coming decades.

The thinkers who wrote in poverty and obscurity, holding nothing but their commitment to truth, produced the moral foundations this platform is built on. Eastern Europe has produced its own version of that tradition — governance knowledge generated under conditions of suppression, revolution, and imperfect freedom, by people who understood what was at stake because they lived it.

It is time for that knowledge to move from the periphery to the center of the conversations that will determine how the 21st century is governed.

That movement starts here.
