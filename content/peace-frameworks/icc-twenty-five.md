# The ICC at Twenty-Five: Justice for Some, Impunity for Others

*worldlegalservices.com — Global Peace Frameworks Series, Article 2*

---

The International Criminal Court was established by the Rome Statute in 1998 and began operations in 2002 with a mandate that represented the most ambitious attempt in human history to hold individuals criminally accountable for the worst crimes — genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression — regardless of their official position or the power of the state they served.

The ambition was genuine and the institutional achievement was real. For the first time, a permanent international criminal tribunal existed with jurisdiction over individuals, not merely states — a court that could, in principle, prosecute a sitting head of state for ordering mass atrocities.

Twenty-five years of operation have clarified the distance between that principle and the practice it has produced. The ICC has delivered justice in some cases. It has demonstrated, through its pattern of operations, a selectivity that reflects the distribution of global power rather than the distribution of international crimes. And it has been systematically undermined by the states whose cooperation it most needs and whose nationals it has been most reluctant to pursue.

---

## What the ICC Has Done

The Court's record is not empty. It has concluded proceedings resulting in convictions in cases from the Democratic Republic of Congo, Uganda, Mali, Central African Republic, and Côte d'Ivoire. It has indicted and in some cases arrested militia leaders, rebel commanders, and government officials responsible for documented atrocities. In the Congolese cases particularly, it has demonstrated that individuals who believed themselves beyond the reach of international justice could be prosecuted and convicted.

The arrest warrant issued for Russian President Vladimir Putin in March 2023 — for the unlawful deportation of Ukrainian children — was a historically significant act: the first time the Court had issued an arrest warrant for the sitting head of state of a permanent Security Council member. Whatever its practical enforcement limitations, the warrant established a legal record that will outlast the political circumstances of its issuance.

These are genuine institutional achievements. They should be acknowledged before the critique that follows.

---

## The African Bias Problem

The ICC's most persistent and most damaging criticism — made most forcefully by the African Union — is that its prosecutorial attention has been concentrated overwhelmingly on African defendants for African crimes, while comparable or worse crimes committed by nationals of powerful Western states or their allies have gone uninvestigated or been investigated and quietly dropped.

The statistics support the concern. Of the Court's first two decades of completed cases, virtually all involved African defendants. The investigations into alleged crimes in Afghanistan — which would have potentially encompassed US military and CIA personnel — were opened, subjected to intense US political and legal pressure, and eventually narrowed in ways that removed the most politically sensitive potential defendants from their scope.

The investigation into the situation in Palestine — opened after the Court's Pre-Trial Chamber confirmed Palestinian standing — has proceeded under conditions of extreme political difficulty, with the Court's judges and prosecutors subject to personal sanctions by the United States government in 2020 under an executive order that was later revoked but whose chilling effect on the institution was real and documented.

The investigation into the situation in Ukraine has proceeded more rapidly and with greater political support from Western states than many previous investigations — which is welcome, but also illustrates how political tailwinds shape institutional pace in ways that undermine the claim of universal jurisdiction applied without fear or favour.

---

## The Enforcement Gap

The ICC has no police force. It cannot arrest anyone. It depends entirely on state cooperation for the execution of its arrest warrants — and states cooperate with ICC warrants when it is politically convenient and decline when it is not.

Omar al-Bashir, the former President of Sudan, was indicted by the ICC in 2009 for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity in Darfur. He travelled freely to dozens of countries — including ICC member states — for years after his indictment, with states simply declining to arrest him. He was eventually removed from power by a domestic coup in 2019, not by international justice.

The pattern is consistent: ICC arrest warrants are executed against the weak, against those who have lost domestic political protection, against those whose transfer serves the political interests of states with the capacity to effect it. They are not executed against powerful incumbents whose detention would require the willing cooperation of states that calculate the political cost as too high.

This is not a failure of the ICC as an institution. It is a structural consequence of building an international criminal court without an international enforcement mechanism — without the capacity to compel state cooperation that domestic courts take for granted.

---

## The Great Power Exemption

Three of the world's most consequential military powers — the United States, Russia, and China — are not members of the ICC. They have not ratified the Rome Statute and do not accept the Court's jurisdiction over their nationals except in limited circumstances.

The United States signed the Rome Statute in 2000 but unsigned it in 2002 under the Bush administration, which was concerned about exposure of US military and intelligence personnel to ICC prosecution. It subsequently negotiated a network of bilateral immunity agreements — Article 98 agreements — with other states, committing them not to surrender US nationals to the Court.

Russia signed the Rome Statute but never ratified it and formally withdrew its signature in 2016 following ICC findings on the Crimea situation.

China has never signed.

The result is a court with universal jurisdiction in principle and a significant carve-out for the states whose nationals are most likely to be involved in the conflicts that generate the Court's most significant potential cases. The crime of aggression — added to the Court's jurisdiction in 2018 — requires the aggressor state to be a Rome Statute member for the Court to have jurisdiction, effectively exempting the US, Russia, and China from prosecution for the crime of launching aggressive wars.

---

## What Reform Requires

Reforming the ICC to close the gap between its mandate and its performance requires confronting several structural problems that have no easy solution.

**Enforcement capacity** without state cooperation requires either an international police force — which no state is willing to create and fund — or significantly stronger political commitment from the states that do cooperate to execute warrants regardless of political cost. The latter requires treating ICC compliance as a genuine obligation of membership rather than an option exercised when convenient.

**Prosecutorial independence** from political pressure requires institutional structures that insulate the Prosecutor's Office from the diplomatic and economic leverage that powerful states apply when investigations approach their nationals. The sanctions applied by the US in 2020 demonstrated the vulnerability of the institution to exactly this pressure.

**Universal membership** is the prerequisite for universal jurisdiction in any meaningful sense. The path to US, Russian, and Chinese membership runs through a negotiation about the conditions under which they would accept the Court's jurisdiction — which means, realistically, a negotiation about the safeguards and limitations that would make membership politically survivable for governments that have historically operated with impunity for their military and intelligence activities.

That negotiation has never seriously begun. It is unlikely to begin under current geopolitical conditions. It is the conversation that must happen before the ICC can become what it was designed to be.

---

## The Value of the Imperfect Institution

Despite everything, the ICC matters — not primarily for what it has achieved but for what it represents: the institutionalisation of the principle that power does not exempt from accountability, that crimes committed by officials of states are crimes committed by individuals who can be named, prosecuted, and convicted.

That principle is contested every day by states that prefer a world in which their leaders operate without legal constraint. The existence of the Court — imperfect, politically constrained, selectively effective — is a continuous institutional argument against that preference.

The argument is worth making even when it cannot be fully enforced. The history of human rights law is the history of principles that were stated before they could be enforced, that shaped behaviour and expectation before they produced reliable accountability, and that eventually — incompletely, unevenly — changed what was possible.

The ICC is at an early stage of that trajectory. Whether it continues to develop toward genuine universal accountability or calcifies into a tool of selective justice applied to the weak depends on choices being made now — by states, by civil society, by the legal community that is the institution's primary constituency.

Those choices deserve to be made with clear eyes about the gap between the institution's current performance and its founding promise.
