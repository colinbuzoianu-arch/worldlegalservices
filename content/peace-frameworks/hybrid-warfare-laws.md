# Hybrid Warfare and the Laws of War: A Framework Built for a Different Century

*worldlegalservices.com — Global Peace Frameworks Series, Article 4*

---

On the morning of December 14, 2020, it became public that the US Treasury Department, the Commerce Department, the Department of Homeland Security, and parts of the State Department had been comprehensively penetrated by a foreign intelligence operation. The attackers had inserted malicious code into a software update from SolarWinds, a network management company, and used it to access the networks of approximately 18,000 organisations — including the most sensitive agencies of the most powerful government in the world — for months without detection.

The operation was attributed, with high confidence, to the Russian Foreign Intelligence Service. It represented arguably the most consequential intelligence penetration in American history. It compromised the communications of government officials, exposed classified programs, and provided Russia with access to the internal deliberations of the US national security apparatus at a level of detail no human spy network could have achieved.

Under the laws of armed conflict, the SolarWinds operation was not an act of war.

This is not a controversial legal conclusion. It is the application of the existing framework to the facts. The operation caused no physical destruction, killed no one, and fell short of the threshold that international law recognises as the use of force. The fact that it was more operationally consequential than many conventional military operations is irrelevant to its legal classification.

This is the hybrid warfare problem in its starkest form: the most consequential tools of modern conflict operate in legal categories that were designed for a world where conflict meant armies, artillery, and territory.

---

## What Hybrid Warfare Is

The term "hybrid warfare" is used imprecisely in both policy and academic discourse, but its core meaning is consistent: the use of a combination of military and non-military means — below the threshold of conventional armed conflict or alongside it — to achieve strategic objectives while maintaining plausible deniability and avoiding the legal and political consequences of open war.

The toolkit includes:

**Cyberattacks** on critical infrastructure — power grids, water systems, financial systems, government networks — that can cause significant harm without firing a shot. The 2015 and 2016 attacks on Ukraine's power grid, attributed to Russian military intelligence, caused widespread blackouts in winter conditions. They were not acts of armed attack under international law as currently interpreted.

**Disinformation operations** designed to destabilise democratic institutions, amplify social divisions, undermine trust in electoral systems, and shift political outcomes. The interference operations documented in the 2016 US presidential election, the Brexit referendum, and elections across Europe were consequential, systematic, and largely beyond the reach of international legal prohibition.

**Economic coercion** — the use of trade restrictions, financial sanctions, debt leverage, and infrastructure dependency to constrain the policy choices of target states. China's use of trade pressure against Australia, Lithuania, and others following political disagreements is a contemporary example. International trade law provides some constraints, but the use of economic instruments as tools of political coercion is poorly addressed by existing frameworks.

**Proxy warfare** — the support of non-state armed groups to advance state interests while maintaining deniability for the supporting state's direct involvement. Russia's support for separatist forces in eastern Ukraine from 2014, Iran's support for Hezbollah and the Houthi movement, the United States' support for armed opposition groups in Syria — all involve the use of non-state actors to pursue state objectives in ways that complicate attribution and legal response.

**Weaponisation of migration** — the deliberate facilitation of irregular migration across borders as a tool of political pressure. Belarus's 2021 operation facilitating the movement of migrants from the Middle East to the Polish border was widely recognised as a hybrid attack on the EU, but the legal framework for responding to it was entirely unclear.

## Why International Law Struggles

International humanitarian law — the laws of armed conflict — was codified primarily in the Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols of 1977. It was designed for the conduct of armed conflict between states — the protection of civilians, the treatment of prisoners, the limits on methods and means of warfare.

The threshold question — what constitutes an "armed attack" sufficient to trigger the right of self-defence under Article 51 of the UN Charter — is defined by reference to the Nicaragua case decided by the International Court of Justice in 1986, which established that not every use of force constitutes an armed attack. The gravity and scale of the operation matter. A cyberattack that causes economic damage but no physical destruction, a disinformation operation that influences an election, an economic coercion campaign that imposes significant harm — none of these clearly meet the armed attack threshold as currently interpreted.

The result is a framework with a significant gap: actions that are more consequential than many conventional military operations, conducted by state actors with strategic intent, fall below the legal threshold that would trigger the right of response and the accountability mechanisms of international law.

States operating in this gap have done so deliberately and systematically. The doctrine of operations below the threshold of armed conflict — called "grey zone" operations in US military doctrine — is explicitly designed to achieve strategic objectives while avoiding the legal and political consequences of actions that would cross the threshold.

---

## The Attribution Problem

Even when hybrid warfare operations cross legal thresholds, the attribution problem creates a second layer of legal difficulty. International law generally requires attributing an action to a state before state responsibility — and therefore the right of response — attaches.

Cyberattacks are designed to obscure attribution. The use of proxy actors, false flag operations, and routing through third-country infrastructure makes definitive attribution technically difficult and legally contested. A state that has been attacked can rarely produce the kind of clear, public, legally admissible evidence of attribution that would justify a response under international law — even when the intelligence community's confidence in attribution is high.

The mismatch between operational intelligence certainty and legal evidence standards is not a technical problem. It is a structural feature of operations specifically designed to exploit it.

---

## Emerging Legal Responses

The absence of a comprehensive legal framework for hybrid warfare does not mean the field is entirely ungoverned. Several developments represent partial responses to the challenge.

The **Tallinn Manual** — a non-binding academic study on how international law applies to cyber operations, now in its second edition — has developed a detailed analytical framework for the legal classification of cyberattacks. It is influential in state practice and has shaped the emerging customary international law of cyber conflict, even without formal legal status.

**EU hybrid warfare frameworks** — including the Joint Framework on Countering Hybrid Threats and the EU Hybrid Toolbox — have developed response mechanisms that include attribution decisions, diplomatic measures, and in principle defensive cyber operations. They represent the most developed regional response to hybrid warfare, though their enforcement capacity remains limited.

**The law of state responsibility** provides some basis for holding states accountable for hybrid operations even below the armed attack threshold — the obligation not to allow territory to be used for operations harmful to other states, the prohibition on interference in the internal affairs of other states, the obligation not to use internationally wrongful acts as instruments of policy. These principles are real, but their application to hybrid warfare operations requires legal development that is still in early stages.

---

## What Is Needed

A legal framework adequate for hybrid warfare requires progress on three fronts simultaneously.

**A revised threshold doctrine.** The armed attack threshold needs to be revisited and refined to account for cyber operations and other hybrid tools that can cause damage equivalent to or exceeding conventional military attacks without meeting the current threshold's criteria. The ICJ's Nicaragua-era threshold was developed for a different operational environment and should be updated through either treaty or the development of customary law through consistent state practice.

**Attribution standards.** International law needs to develop standards for the attribution of hybrid operations that are consistent with the intelligence realities of the field — allowing states to invoke legal response rights based on intelligence attribution that meets a defined evidentiary standard, rather than requiring the kind of public, legally admissible proof that the design of hybrid operations specifically prevents.

**Accountability for sub-threshold operations.** The current framework's failure to provide accountability for sub-threshold hybrid operations creates a systematic incentive to conduct them. A framework that imposes meaningful consequences — diplomatic, economic, legal — for documented hybrid operations, even below the armed attack threshold, would reshape the cost-benefit calculation that makes these operations attractive.

---

## The Strategic Reality

Hybrid warfare is not a temporary phenomenon that will recede when international relations improve. It is the stable operating mode of great power competition in a world where direct conventional conflict between nuclear-armed states is too costly and too risky to be the primary instrument of strategic competition.

The states best positioned to conduct hybrid warfare — those with the most developed cyber capabilities, the most sophisticated information operations, the deepest economic leverage — are also the states most resistant to legal frameworks that would constrain those capabilities.

This is the political economy of hybrid warfare law: the states whose behaviour most needs to be constrained have the most power to prevent the development of constraining frameworks. It is the same dynamic that blocks nuclear disarmament, ICC reform, and Security Council veto limitation.

Breaking that dynamic requires the kind of sustained multilateral pressure, civil society mobilisation, and legal institution building that produced the Ottawa Treaty on landmines and the Rome Statute — neither of which was achieved through the cooperation of the most powerful states, but both of which changed the normative landscape in ways that eventually constrained even reluctant actors.

The legal framework for hybrid warfare needs to be built the same way: from outside the great power consensus, by states and civil society organisations that understand the costs of the current vacuum and have the political will to begin filling it.
