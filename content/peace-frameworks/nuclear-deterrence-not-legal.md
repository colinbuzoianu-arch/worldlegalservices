# Nuclear Deterrence Is Not a Legal Framework. It Is the Absence of One.

*worldlegalservices.com — Global Peace Frameworks Series, Article 3*

---

There are approximately 12,500 nuclear warheads in existence. Nine states possess them. Each of those states has developed, to varying degrees of formality, a doctrine governing the conditions under which it would use them.

None of those doctrines is subject to binding international legal constraint. None has been negotiated through a democratic process that gave the people most at risk — which is to say, everyone — a meaningful voice. None can be challenged in any international court. None generates any legal accountability for the officials who wrote them or the governments that maintain them.

Nuclear deterrence — the strategic doctrine that has governed the relationship between nuclear-armed states since 1945 — is not a legal framework. It is the organised absence of one: a set of strategic calculations, operational plans, and political commitments that exist entirely outside the architecture of international law and that could, if they fail, produce consequences that would dwarf every other catastrophe in human history.

This is the most consequential legal vacuum in existence. It receives less serious policy attention than the regulation of food labelling.

---

## What Deterrence Actually Is

Nuclear deterrence, in its core formulation, is the claim that the possession of nuclear weapons prevents their use — that the certainty of devastating retaliation creates a stable equilibrium in which rational actors will not initiate nuclear conflict because the consequences would be self-destructive.

The theory has a distinguished intellectual history and genuine strategic logic. During the Cold War, the mutual assured destruction framework — in which the US and Soviet Union each maintained second-strike capability sufficient to annihilate the other even after absorbing a first strike — produced a strategic stability of sorts. The great powers did not fight each other directly. The nuclear threshold was not crossed.

Whether this stability was the product of deterrence doctrine or of a combination of luck, miscommunication avoidance, and the restraint of specific individuals at specific moments of crisis is a question that strategic historians continue to debate. The Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 — the closest the world has come to nuclear exchange — was resolved not through the smooth operation of deterrence theory but through back-channel diplomacy, unilateral concessions, and the intervention of individuals whose decisions were not part of any strategic doctrine.

What is not seriously contested is that deterrence, as a strategy for preventing nuclear war, depends on a set of conditions — rational actors, reliable communication, accurate intelligence, stable command and control — that are not guaranteed and have repeatedly come close to failing.

---

## The Legal Status of Nuclear Weapons

The international legal framework governing nuclear weapons is thin, contested, and largely unenforceable.

The **Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT)**, signed in 1968 and now ratified by 191 states, establishes three pillars: non-proliferation (non-nuclear states agree not to acquire nuclear weapons), disarmament (nuclear states commit to good-faith negotiations toward eventual disarmament), and peaceful use of nuclear technology. The NPT has achieved real non-proliferation success — the number of nuclear-armed states has grown far less than was feared in 1968. It has achieved essentially no progress on disarmament. The nuclear-armed states have consistently interpreted their Article VI disarmament obligation as aspirational rather than legally binding, and no mechanism exists to compel compliance.

The **Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW)**, which entered into force in 2021, goes further: it prohibits the development, testing, production, stockpiling, transfer, use, and threatened use of nuclear weapons for states parties. It has been ratified by 73 states. Not one of the nine nuclear-armed states has signed it. The treaty is therefore legally binding on none of the states whose behaviour it was designed to constrain.

The **International Court of Justice** issued an advisory opinion in 1996 concluding that the use of nuclear weapons would generally be contrary to international humanitarian law, but could not conclude that it would be unlawful in all circumstances — particularly in extreme cases of self-defence where the survival of a state was at stake. The opinion was legally non-binding and has not been applied in any subsequent legal proceeding.

The result: nuclear weapons are the most destructive instruments of violence in human history, and their use is governed by a legal framework that amounts to: probably illegal in most circumstances, definitely illegal in some, possibly legal in others, and completely unenforceable in any.

## The Modernisation Paradox

While the legal framework has stagnated, the nuclear arsenals of the major powers have undergone significant modernisation. The United States, Russia, and China are all in the midst of multi-decade nuclear modernisation programs that will result in smaller, more accurate, more survivable, and in some cases more usable nuclear weapons.

The concept of "usability" in the nuclear context deserves unpacking. Cold War deterrence doctrine was built around weapons so large and so indiscriminate that their use was almost inconceivable in any limited military context — a feature, not a bug, because it made the nuclear threshold psychologically high. Modern tactical nuclear weapons — smaller yield, more precise, deployable in battlefield contexts — lower the threshold by making nuclear use more conceivable as a military option rather than a civilisational catastrophe.

This is the modernisation paradox: the weapons are getting better at the same time that the doctrines, legal frameworks, and international institutions governing them are getting weaker. The gap between what the weapons can do and what the law can say about them is widening.

## The New Nuclear Landscape

The bipolar nuclear deterrence of the Cold War has given way to a multipolar landscape that deterrence theory handles much less confidently.

In a bipolar framework, the strategic calculation is relatively straightforward: each side maintains second-strike capability, neither side gains from first use, the equilibrium is stable. In a multipolar framework — with the US, Russia, China, India, Pakistan, North Korea, Israel, France, and the UK all possessing nuclear weapons, with different doctrines, different command structures, different communication channels, and different strategic calculations — the stability of the deterrence equilibrium is considerably less certain.

The India-Pakistan dyad is the nuclear relationship most at risk of escalation. Two nuclear-armed states with a history of armed conflict, disputed territory, domestic political pressures that reward nationalist posturing, and relatively limited crisis communication infrastructure. The deterrence theory that holds in Washington and Moscow depends on conditions — reliable communication, rational leadership, stable command and control — that cannot be assumed to hold in every crisis between Islamabad and New Delhi.

North Korea's nuclear program represents a further complication: a state whose leadership calculates that nuclear weapons are essential to regime survival and whose strategic doctrine — deliberately opaque and designed to generate maximum uncertainty — is precisely designed to undermine the predictability that deterrence theory requires.

## What a Legal Framework Would Require

A genuine legal framework for nuclear weapons — one that goes beyond the aspirational disarmament commitments of the NPT and the non-binding prohibitions of the TPNW — would need to address questions that no existing instrument approaches.

**Use doctrine transparency.** States that possess nuclear weapons should be required to publish their use doctrines — the conditions under which they would consider nuclear use — in sufficient detail for independent legal assessment of their consistency with international humanitarian law. Current doctrines range from partially disclosed to entirely secret.

**First use prohibition.** A binding international prohibition on the first use of nuclear weapons — committing nuclear-armed states not to use nuclear weapons against a state that has not used them first — would not eliminate the nuclear threat but would narrow the scenarios in which nuclear use is contemplated. Several states already maintain no-first-use policies; making this a binding multilateral commitment would represent meaningful progress.

**Command and control standards.** International standards for nuclear command and control — designed to minimise the risk of accidental launch, unauthorised use, or launch based on false warning — should be subject to international verification. The Accidents Measures Agreement between the US and USSR was a model of this kind of technical cooperation; updating and expanding it to the multipolar context is feasible and urgent.

**Genuine disarmament timelines.** The NPT's Article VI commitment to disarmament negotiations needs to be operationalised with binding timelines, independent verification, and accountability mechanisms that treat the commitment as a legal obligation rather than a diplomatic aspiration.

None of this is politically achievable in the short term. All of it is more achievable than continuing with a framework in which 12,500 weapons of mass destruction are governed by nothing more binding than the strategic calculations of nine governments.

---

## The Honest Assessment

Nuclear deterrence has not failed — in the sense that nuclear weapons have not been used in conflict since 1945. Whether this record reflects the success of deterrence theory or the good fortune of specific individuals making specific decisions under extreme pressure at specific moments of crisis is a question that cannot be definitively answered.

What can be said is that the framework governing the most dangerous weapons in human history consists of a non-proliferation treaty whose disarmament pillar has never been implemented, a prohibition treaty that none of the armed states have signed, an ICJ advisory opinion that establishes probable illegality without enforceability, and a collection of bilateral and multilateral confidence-building measures that reduce risk at the margins without addressing it structurally.

This is not a legal framework. It is the organised hope that the weapons will not be used.

Hope is not architecture. And the consequences of its failure are not recoverable.
